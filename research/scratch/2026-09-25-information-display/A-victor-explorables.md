# Lane A — Bret Victor + explorable-explanation lineage → /figure principles

**Verdict:** For *displaying* information, the Victor lineage and its disconfirmers point the same way: design the static graphic first, put everything needed for the claim in the default view, and treat interaction/animation as optional depth that most readers never use (NYT: ~85% did not click a prominent button). The Victor ideas that hold up are show-the-state, abstract-over-a-variable plus one highlighted concrete case, side-by-side variants, and linked multiple representations. The claim that interactivity or animation helps understanding has thin empirical support. Robertson 2008 found static small multiples beat animation for analysis, and Tversky 2002 found that animation's apparent wins came from confounds.

Scope: extract display/figure/explanation principles (not interactive-tool design, which
/interface-thinking already covers: directness, hidden-state, ladder-as-tool-lens, constraints).

## Principles

### P1. Design the static graphic first; add interaction only for context you cannot infer or print
- **Rule:** Before adding a slider, tooltip or toggle, ask what the reader's questions are and whether one information-rich static graphic answers them. Interaction is a last resort, used only when the data space is too large to print and the reader's context (their row, their scenario) cannot be inferred. Every interaction you do add must visibly change the graphic.
- **Source:** Victor, *Magic Ink* (2006), https://worrydream.com/MagicInk/ — READ directly (sections "Information software design is graphic design", "Interactivity considered harmful", "Reducing interaction"). Verified quotes: "I suggest that the design of information software should be approached initially and primarily as a graphic design project."; "Interaction should be used _judiciously_ and _sparingly_, only when the environment and history provide insufficient context to construct an acceptable graphic."; "each interaction can and should result in a discernible change to a context-sensitive information graphic." Also the baseline test: "If information software can't present its data at least as well as a piece of paper, how have we progressed?"
- **Evidence type:** argument (design essay with worked redesigns; no user study).
- **Exemplar:** Magic Ink's own redesigns of the Amazon search page and Yahoo movie listings (static graphics that answer the reader's questions without clicks); Marey/Ibry graphical train timetable, which Victor uses as the model case.
- **Does NOT apply:** Manipulation/authoring tools (Victor scopes this to information software); games/play-to-learn, which he names as "a radically alternative approach"; and explorables whose point is letting the reader test a model (see P6).

### P2. Answer the reader's questions by placement: put the compared things side by side on the axes of the main question
- **Rule:** Write the reader's 2-5 questions, then pick the two spatial axes from the primary question (e.g. "which movies, at which times" → movies × time). Never make the reader compare across clicks or pages: "navigate by _hand_ instead of by _eye_, and ... use her memory to compare information across _time_ instead of _space_" is the failure. Replace a mean with its distribution when the mean hides a decision-relevant split.
- **Source:** *Magic Ink*, "Demonstration: Showing the data" and "Arranging the data" — READ. Quote verified above; also "Just as important as _what_ data is shown is _where_ it is shown." The 5-star-with-whiskers redesign distinguishes a book "unanimously judged middling" from one "loved and hated".
- **Evidence type:** argument / craft canon (Victor explicitly builds on Tufte's "Show the data").
- **Exemplar:** Graphical train timetable (distance × time; crossings pop out because "the eye is naturally drawn toward line intersections"); Victor's movie-by-time grid redesign.
- **Does NOT apply:** When the reader needs exact lookups of a few values (a table wins; /figure step 2 already says so), or when the reader is an agent.

### P3. Anchor the graphic on the reader's "now/here/me" landmark
- **Rule:** If the data has a time or space axis and the reader's position on it is known, mark it (shade the past, highlight the reader's region/row/scenario) so the eye has a springboard. For agent-made figures, the analogue is: highlight the case the reader asked about (their country, their model, the main specification) and let the rest be context.
- **Source:** *Magic Ink*, "Inferring context from the environment" — READ. Verified: "in any data space with a temporal dimension, 'now' is almost always the prime landmark"; movie-grid redesign: "The current time is indicated by shading the past, providing a springboard for the viewer's eye".
- **Evidence type:** argument.
- **Exemplar:** Victor's movie-showtimes grid with past shaded; NYT "You Draw It" family-income pieces that put the reader's guess on the chart (NYT 2015/2017) — exemplar named from training knowledge, not re-verified in this pass.
- **Does NOT apply:** Figures meant to be read identically by every reader (a paper figure), where no single "now/me" exists; then highlight the author's main case instead.

### P4. Show the state at each step; don't make the reader "play computer"
- **Rule:** When you explain a process, algorithm or model, draw the system's state at each step next to the sentence that describes that step. Words carry structure; pictures carry behavior. Couple picture to sentence (fine-grained) when explaining mechanics; couple picture to paragraph when the picture is an example of a concept.
- **Source:** Victor, *Scientific Communication As Sequential Art* (2011), https://worrydream.com/ScientificCommunicationAsSequentialArt/ — READ. Verified: "When an algorithm is described in prose (or code), we are typically given only the rules of the system -- we can't see the data or the state. In order to understand what the algorithm is doing, we have to 'play computer' and imagine the state in our head." Also: "Paradoxically, making lavish use of space can result in briefer writing -- illustrations allow for terser prose." *Media for Thinking the Unthinkable* (2013), https://worrydream.com/MediaForThinkingTheUnthinkable/ — READ: "Words describe the **structure** of the algorithm, and pictures depict the **behavior** of the algorithm."
- **Evidence type:** argument / craft demonstration (redesign of Watts & Strogatz 1998 Nature paper).
- **Exemplar:** Victor's comic-style redesign of Watts & Strogatz "Collective dynamics of 'small-world' networks"; Amit Patel's redblobgames A* / hex-grid pages (see P10).
- **Does NOT apply:** When the reader already knows the procedure (Victor makes the step-through optional for this reason) or the process has one step.

### P5. Climb the ladder: show the whole family (all times / all parameters) and let the reader drop to one concrete case
- **Rule:** When a figure depicts one run, one time, or one parameter value, ask whether a representation that abstracts over that variable (full trajectory instead of a snapshot, overlaid runs, a metric-vs-parameter plot, a heatmap over two parameters) shows the pattern better. Then always pair it with one concrete instance highlighted on top (the "step down"), because abstractions reveal patterns but cannot explain them. For a static figure: overlay one highlighted run on the faint family; or put the abstract panel and one concrete panel side by side.
- **Source:** Victor, *Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction* (2011), https://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/ — READ. Verified: "we can go higher, and concoct a representation that statically depicts the system _across all time_."; "These representations reveal interesting high-level patterns. But they are too abstract to _explain_ those patterns."; "stepping _down_ is as important as stepping _up_." He also gives the three options for a family whose members live in different environments: overlap (confusing when frames differ), small multiples ("because each trajectory is separate and isolated, it's difficult to make _visual comparisons_ between them"), or a coordinate transform into a shared space.
- **Evidence type:** argument (interactive essay). Overlap with /interface-thinking `ladder` lens: that lens asks whether a *tool* supports switching; this rule is about which *figure* to draw.
- **Exemplar:** The essay's car-trajectory overlays and the turning-rate × bend-angle heatmap with a highlighted slice; specification curves in the /figure skill are an instance (all specs, main one marked).
- **Does NOT apply:** When the claim is about one case (a single estimate, one event); when the abstraction needs a coordinate transform the reader will not follow — Victor admits his warped-trajectory view is "too abstract to make sense of" on its own.

### P6. If you make it explorable, the static reading must already work, and the author picks the examples
- **Rule:** An interactive or parameterised figure must read correctly with every control at its default: default values set to the author's main case, and the text states the takeaway. Interaction goes where a reader would ask "what if?" about an assumption (the knob is the assumption), not on decorative dimensions. Never ship a sandbox ("figure it out yourself") in place of an explanation.
- **Source:** Victor, *Explorable Explanations* (2011, postscript 2024), https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/ — READ. Verified: "the filter description works as a static explanation -- it can be **read like normal text**. The reader is not _forced_ to interact in order to learn."; "Most interactive widgets dump the user in a sandbox and say 'figure it out for yourself'. _Those are not explanations._"; "the author holds up their end of the conversation". Reactive document "can be read at multiple levels ... The hurried reader can skim it. The casual reader can read it as-is. The curious reader can adjust the author's scenarios." 2024 postscript narrows the intent: "a written _argument_ whose assertions are backed by explorable computational models, whose _facts, assumptions, and calculations_ are all visible and editable."
- **Evidence type:** argument; corroborated by practitioner data in P8 (most readers never touch the controls, so the default view IS the figure for most of them).
- **Exemplar:** Victor's Prop 21 reactive paragraph and *Ten Brighter Ideas*; Gregor Aisch's NYT GOP-delegate scenario sliders (cited in Aisch 2017, see P8).
- **Does NOT apply:** When no assumption is genuinely uncertain or reader-dependent — then a knob is decoration. Also agent readers (they read the default/serialised state only).

### P7. Show several linked representations of the same behaviour at once, not one "best" chart
- **Rule:** When the reader must build intuition for a system (filter, model, estimator), place 2-6 representations of the same state together (e.g. time series + distribution + parameter-space position + formula), with shared highlighting so a point in one marks the same case in the others. In static media: same case highlighted in the same colour across panels.
- **Source:** *Explorable Explanations* §2 — READ ("the figure's use of **multiple representations**. We are shown _six_ different ways of characterizing the filter ... Each representation gives a unique insight."); *Media for Thinking the Unthinkable* — READ (Demo 3 "As the designer adjusts parameters, five different ways of interpreting the behavior are shown simultaneously"; Demo 5 "Pointing to any object highlights **where it came from** in previous stages, **where it went** in later stages"). Hohman et al. 2020 (Distill) summarise the same affordance: "Utilizing multiple representations allows a reader to see different abstractions of a single idea" — READ.
- **Evidence type:** argument / craft demonstration (no controlled comparison in these sources). Multimedia-learning literature on multiple representations is not reviewed in this lane.
- **Exemplar:** Victor's state-variable-filter figure (Bode plot, pole-zero plot, impulse response, etc.); Distill "Why Momentum Really Works" (Goh 2017), which links an equation layout to a live optimisation plot.
- **Does NOT apply:** When the reader only needs one lookup or one claim (/figure step 2 routes that to a sentence or table); novices without the vocabulary for each view get overloaded — introduce each representation before linking them.

### P8. Never hide content the reader needs behind a hover, click, tab or slider
- **Rule:** Put every value needed for the claim on the page in the default view (direct labels, annotations, printed numbers). Tooltips/steppers/tabs may only carry optional depth: fact-checking detail, the long tail of a dataset, alternate scenarios. If you ask the reader to do more than scroll, the payoff must be large.
- **Source:** Archie Tse, "Why We Are Doing Fewer Interactives", Malofiej 24 (2016), slides on Tse's own GitHub: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf — READ (pdftotext). **Exact wording** (the brief's paraphrase drops "ever"): "2. If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it. If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it." Rule 1: "If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen." Rule 3: "When deciding whether to make some-thing interactive, remember that getting it to work on all platforms is expensive." Slides also: "Readers just want to scroll."; "Most visuals are static."; "We still do interactives, but the bar is now VERY high." **The slides contain no interaction-rate percentage.** The number comes from Gregor Aisch, "In Defense of Interactive Graphics" (vis4.net, 31 Mar 2017), https://vis4.net/blog/in-defense-of-interactive-graphics/ — READ: the "85%" figure was "me measuring the percent of readers who clicked on a prominent button in a couple of graphics we published in 2015 ... the 85% of readers didn't ignore the graphic, they just didn't click the button." Aisch: "All it means is that you should not _hide_ _important_ _content_ behind interactions ... not everything is crucial and 15% of readers aren't nobody."
- **Evidence type:** practitioner field observation (NYT analytics, n = "a couple of graphics", one button each; not a study). Hohman et al. 2020 note "large publishers are unwilling to share internal metrics" and that this anecdote "has solidified as a rule-of-thumb" perhaps beyond its evidence.
- **Exemplar:** NYT graphics after 2016: static, directly labelled charts with scroll-triggered motion; Aisch's European-election piece where static charts label the key parties and tooltips carry the rest (Aisch 2017).
- **Does NOT apply:** Tools and dashboards for analysts who come to explore (Victor's manipulation/analysis context; /interface-thinking); pieces where the interaction IS the story (You-Draw-It prediction prompts, P11). Also note the 15% who do interact are the fact-checkers: Aisch reports tooltips led to "a sad record number of corrections" — interaction as transparency.

### P9. Prefer static small multiples or traces to animation for comparison; animate only a congruent change, schematically, slowly, and with a pause/step control
- **Rule:** For trends across many entities, draw small multiples (most accurate) or overlaid trace lines, not an animated scatter. Use animation only when (a) the content is itself change over time/causal sequence (congruence), (b) you can make it schematic, slow and annotated with arrows/highlights (apprehension), and (c) the reader can pause, step and replay. For a narrated presentation with a clean story, animation is acceptable; for analysis, it is the worst of the three options tested. If a static figure with arrows or a stepped sequence can show the change, use it.
- **Source:** Robertson, Fernandez, Fisher, Lee & Stasko, "Effectiveness of Animation in Trend Visualization", IEEE TVCG 14(6) 2008, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2008.125 — READ full text (author PDF, Stasko page). Verified abstract: "Animation is the least effective form for analysis; both static depictions of trends are significantly faster than animation, and the small multiples display is more accurate." and animation "is the fastest technique for presentation and participants find it enjoyable and exciting, it does lead to many participant errors." Conclusion: "Presenters showing animated data should strongly consider ensuring that their data tells a clean story: this can be confused by having too many data points, by data points that reverse their tracks over time, or by having points that do not move in synchrony." Presentation arm n=18 (three groups of six); participant quote "the dots flew everywhere". Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt, "Animation: can it facilitate?", IJHCS 57(4):247-262, 2002, doi:10.1006/ijhc.2002.1017 — READ key sections via author-hosted full text (Columbia TC PDF, retrieved as extended Exa highlights; not the typeset Elsevier version). Verified: "In cases where animated graphics seem superior to static ones, scrutiny reveals lack of equivalence between animated and static graphics in content or procedures; the animated graphics convey more information or involve interactivity."; "Animations are often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived. Moreover, many continuous events are conceived of as sequences of discrete steps."; "animations should lean toward the schematic and away from the realistic"; "even for these cases, clever schematization of static diagrams may be just as effective as animation. For example, arrows are effective in indicating temporal sequence and direction of motion".
- **Evidence type:** empirical study (Robertson: lab, ~36 participants, Gapminder-style data; ages 23-53) + review of empirical literature (Tversky: narrative review, not a meta-analysis). Pre-2010 studies, but the mechanism (perceptual tracking load, transience) is scale-independent — no LLM/scale issue here.
- **Exemplar:** Robertson's own small-multiples trend display vs Gapminder Trendalyzer; Muybridge's *Horse in Motion* stills (Hohman et al. 2020: still series better for "Does a horse lift all four of its feet off the ground", animation better for a "visceral sense").
- **Does NOT apply:** Real-time reorientation in space/time (Tversky's named exception); manner-of-motion and microstep timing where animation may carry information statics lack (Tversky: "unknown" whether better); narrated talks with a clean story (Robertson: fastest for presentation). /manim-animations is the right tool when these hold.

### P10. Show all the variants side by side instead of making the reader toggle; give every diagram a useful initial state
- **Rule:** When you are tempted to add a toggle/tab/slider to switch between variants (method A vs B, cube vs axial, scenario 1 vs 2), draw the variants next to each other instead. Where a diagram must stay interactive, its first frame must already teach something; label the static frame (labels, arrows) because readers who never press play see only that frame. Pick the diagram the reader needs, not the one your reusable chart component makes easy.
- **Source:** Amit Patel (Red Blob Games), "Little Design Things" (2019), https://www.redblobgames.com/making-of/little-things/ — READ via Exa extended highlights (partial page text), verified: "It's often better to show all the variants than to ask the reader to interact with the diagram to see the variants. Interactivity isn't always needed."; "when feasible, start a diagram in a \"useful\" state that doesn't require interaction to learn from it"; "ideally my pages will work as static pages without needing any interactivity". Patel, "Graph search diagrams and reusable code" (simblob, 29 Apr 2020), https://simblob.blogspot.com/2020/04/graph-search-diagrams-and-reusable-code.html — READ via highlights: "Back when I wrote this page, I wanted every diagram to be interactive. I had convinced myself that interaction was always better. Since then I've realized that interaction makes some things worse."; "If most readers don't interact with the diagrams, making this interactive has made it worse."; "I was implementing the best diagram I could given the abstraction, rather than the best diagram for the reader." Follow-up (Aug 2020) re-tied the animation slider to *distance from start* (the concept) instead of loop iterations (the implementation).
- **Evidence type:** craft canon (20-year practitioner's self-critique of his own pages).
- **Exemplar:** Red Blob Games "Introduction to A*" side-by-side BFS vs Dijkstra vs Greedy vs A* comparisons; "Hexagonal Grids" page.
- **Does NOT apply:** When variants number in the dozens (then small multiples with a highlighted main case, or a parameter-space plot per P5); when the variant IS the reader's own input.

### P11. Order figures in an explanation concrete-first, question-first, and connect them with "but/therefore"
- **Rule:** Open with the question the reader cares about, then show one concrete instance before any aggregate; each subsequent figure should answer a "but ..." raised by the previous one. Put the open-ended sandbox/full-data view last, after the reader has the concepts. For a multi-figure page, write the chain of takeaways ("X, BUT Y, THEREFORE Z") before drawing.
- **Source:** Nicky Case, "How I Make Explorable Explanations" (blog.ncase.me, 20 Sep 2017), https://blog.ncase.me/how-i-make-an-explorable-explanation/ — READ. Verified: "you've got to make them love your question."; "**start on the ground**. The very first thing you should do is give the reader a _concrete experience_."; "**move up, step by step.**"; Stone & Parker structure "this happens, THEREFORE that happens, BUT that happens"; "In the beginning, I start by giving the player _my_ question. And at the end, I want them to explore their _own_ questions." Case credits Victor's Ladder essay as inspiring "like, 90% of my work".
- **Evidence type:** craft canon (argument from practice; no measurement).
- **Exemplar:** Nicky Case, *Parable of the Polygons* (with Vi Hart) and *The Evolution of Trust* — concrete play first, sandbox last.
- **Does NOT apply:** Reference/lookup documents and executive summaries, where the answer must come first (the /figure "claim first" title still holds — Case's question-first is about narrative order, not about hiding the takeaway); single-figure answers.

### P12. Treat a figure as a transformation of the idea, not polish; if you cannot find a clear figure, suspect your understanding
- **Rule:** When no candidate figure makes the claim obvious, do not add decoration or more panels; re-derive what the claim actually is (maybe it is a sentence, per /figure step 2) or find the representation in which it becomes visible (a different axis, a transform, a notation). Budget effort by audience: a figure read by many readers justifies far more design effort than a one-off.
- **Source:** Olah & Carter, "Research Debt", Distill 2017, doi:10.23915/distill.00005, https://distill.pub/2017/research-debt/ — READ. Verified: "good explanations often involve transforming the idea. This kind of refinement of an idea can take just as much effort and deep understanding as the initial discovery."; "Developing good abstractions, notations, visualizations, and so forth, is improving the user interfaces for ideas."; "if we can't explain an idea well, that's often a sign that we don't understand it as well as we could."; one-to-many explanations mean "each member of the audience pays the cost of understanding, even though the cost of explaining stays the same." Victor, *Kill Math* — READ: Playfair's line graphs let "everyone ... **feel data through their eyes**"; "a person should not have to imagine the interpretation of abstract symbols" (Victor's contested claim; see below).
- **Evidence type:** argument.
- **Exemplar:** Victor's train timetable (Magic Ink: "This graphic incorporates no more _data_ than the previous one, yet all of the operators' questions are answered at a glance."); Strogatz phase-portrait of a nonlinear ODE (Kill Math anecdote).
- **Does NOT apply:** Throwaway exploratory plots for yourself; here speed beats craft.

## Contested / disconfirming

**1. Animation does not beat an information-equivalent static graphic (Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt 2002).** Verified (author-hosted full text, key sections): "In cases where animated graphics seem superior to static ones, scrutiny reveals lack of equivalence between animated and static graphics in content or procedures; the animated graphics convey more information or involve interactivity." Mechanism: the Apprehension Principle — "Animations are often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived. Moreover, many continuous events are conceived of as sequences of discrete steps." Their prescription is schematic, slow, annotated animation with learner control, and they note arrows in static diagrams may do as well. Scope limit: a narrative review of mostly education/mechanics studies, pre-2002. Later meta-analyses (e.g. Höffler & Leutner 2007, which reported a positive effect for representational animations) are NOT reviewed in this lane. Treat "animation never helps" as overstated. The defensible version is that animation helps no more than a matched static graphic unless the motion itself is the content. **Tension with Victor:** Victor's filter and car examples are parameter *interaction*, not playback animation, which Tversky explicitly exempts as the possible remedy. So these two sources partly agree.

**2. Trend animation (Gapminder-style) vs small multiples (Robertson et al. 2008).** READ full text. For analysis, animation was slowest; small multiples was most accurate. In the presentation arm, animation was fastest, but participants made many errors and still rated it "fun" or "exciting". Participants only "slightly preferred" it, so the fun did not translate into much preference. Caveats: lab study, 18 participants per arm, desktop, 2008; "All three techniques fail to scale beyond about 200 data points." The authors themselves suggest "examining their data with a small multiples view, but then presenting it with animation". That leaves room for a narrated animation when the data "tells a clean story", so the paper does not ban animation.

**3. "Readers don't interact" (Archie Tse, NYT, Malofiej 24, March 2016).** VERIFIED against Tse's own slide deck (GitHub archietse/malofiej-2016). Exact wording: "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it. If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it." The brief's version omits "ever". The slides give NO percentage. The widely repeated "85% ignore interactives" traces to Gregor Aisch (NYT). By his own account it measured clicks on "a prominent button in a couple of graphics we published in 2015". It then passed through talks and Medium posts to FastCo, and Aisch objects that "the 85% of readers didn't ignore the graphic, they just didn't click the button." So the evidence behind the canonical rule is one newsroom, a handful of graphics, and click-through on a single button. It is directionally supported by Patel's self-critique and Tse's own shift to static graphics, but it is not a study. Hohman et al. 2020 (Distill) make the same point: the rule "has solidified as a rule-of-thumb ... designers are potentially choosing a suboptimal presentation of their story due to this anecdote", and "There is limited empirical evaluation of the effectiveness of interactive articles." They also cite contrary work: readers "even those on mobile devices, are interested in utilizing interactivity when it is a core part of the article's message" (study not read here).

**4. Victor's "interaction is a last resort" vs the explorables movement he started.** Magic Ink (2006) argues against interaction for information software. Explorable Explanations (2011) builds reactive documents. The 2024 postscript disowns most pedagogical explorables: "almost all of these articles are pedagogical, and that's not really what I was going for". He reframes the goal as argument backed by editable models, and says he "had already given up on the computer screen as a medium for model-grounded discussion". The resolution that fits /figure: the static graphic carries the claim, and a model's assumptions get knobs only when the reader is meant to test the argument (P6). Explorability here serves transparency, not engagement. Aisch's "tooltips invite fact-checking" point supports this reading.

**5. Scrollytelling vs steppers.** Hohman et al. 2020 cite McKenna et al. (no significant engagement difference between scroll and step layouts; both preferred over static) and Zhi et al. (better comprehension with slideshow than vertical scroll; desktop only). The underlying studies were not read. Treat "use scrollytelling" as unproven.

**6. Kill Math's anti-symbol stance.** Victor: "a person should not have to imagine the interpretation of abstract symbols." This is an argument, not evidence, and it cuts against expert readers, for whom an equation or table is the fastest representation. The /figure rule "reader is an agent → table/JSON" is the same boundary. Do not turn this into "always visualize".

**7. Engagement is not comprehension.** Hohman et al. claim aesthetics and interaction raise engagement and time-on-page, "ultimately lead[ing] to improved learning outcomes". That chain is inferred, not measured end to end. Robertson's result, where animation was rated fun and exciting but produced more errors, is a direct counterexample. For /figure, grade figures on reader accuracy (the blind-read step), never on appeal.
