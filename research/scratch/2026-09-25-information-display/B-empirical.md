# Lane B — empirical visualization/perception literature for /figure

**Verdict:** The empirical literature backs /figure's core moves (claim-as-title, sentence/table/chart by reader task, blind reader) and adds three it lacks: (1) force ONE comparison with grouping, highlight and a direct annotation placed by content type (axis text at the axis, numbers at the point, the trend claim in the title); (2) the title and the chart's most salient feature must agree, since titles steer recall and readers rarely notice slant; (3) show uncertainty as outcomes (quantile dotplot/HOPs), naming the interval. Several famous rules are weaker than their reputation: data-ink/declutter has no reliable performance effect; pies are task-dependent; zero-baseline is a context rule, and truncation still exaggerates on line charts; encoding rankings are population averages that 20-40% of individuals do not follow.

Scope: what the empirical vis/perception literature says about displaying information insightfully (sentence vs chart, form choice, titles/annotation, uncertainty, memorability, encoding ranking), plus a disconfirmation pass on famous rules. Excludes color palettes, marks, tooltips, a11y (owned by /dataviz).

## Findings

### F1. Pick ONE comparison per figure and force it with grouping, highlight and a direct annotation
- **Rule:** Before building, name the single comparison that carries the claim. Put the compared items adjacent (proximity beats colour for grouping), highlight only that set, and write the conclusion as a text annotation next to the pattern it describes. Assume a naive reader will not find the pattern you find.
- **Source:** Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks, Hullman (2021), "The Science of Visual Data Communication: What Works," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 22(3):110-161, doi:10.1177/15291006211051956. **Full text read** (sections on comparison, annotation, curse of knowledge, summary of key guidelines).
- **Design/effect:** Review, not a single study. Its synthesis: extracting global statistics (mean, extrema) is fast, but comparisons between subsets are "limited to only a handful per second"; its summary says to "use visual grouping cues to control which set of comparisons a viewer should make, and use annotation and highlighting to narrow that set to the single most important comparison." Proximity grouping controlled comparisons more strongly than colour grouping (their Fig. 9, citing Hearst et al. 2020). Guidance is stronger for low-knowledge readers (Canham & Hegarty 2010); "less important for experts."
- **Figure decision:** Adds a step between the /figure claim and the form: "which one comparison?" A chart whose claim needs 3+ unhighlighted comparisons is either a small-multiples set or should be split.
- **Limits:** Review-level synthesis; many cited effects come from education/psych lab tasks rather than real reports.

### F2. Replace legends with direct labels; avoid animation except for sampling uncertainty
- **Rule:** Label series at their ends/points instead of a legend. Do not use animated transitions to show change over time in a static-reading context; use small multiples or a static trajectory. Animation is acceptable (preferred) only for showing draws from a distribution (HOPs).
- **Source:** Franconeri et al. 2021 (above), full text, "Beware the working memory load of animation" and summary.
- **Design/effect:** Synthesis. Cited example: students seeing an animated toilet-flush diagram made 20% errors about individual stages vs 5% for static (Kriz & Hegarty 2007), and animated-diagram viewers reported *higher* engagement and *lower* perceived difficulty while recalling less (Paik & Schraw 2013), an illusion of understanding. Interactive Gapminder-style animation helped precise per-country questions but hurt extraction of global trends vs static (Abukhodair et al. 2013; Robertson et al. 2008). "There is little evidence that animation facilitates understanding of information displays," with the stated exception of probabilistic processes.
- **Figure decision:** /figure's tool table should route "change over time" to small multiples/static, and reserve motion (`/manim-animations`, HOPs) for sampling/uncertainty or presenter-narrated talks.
- **Limits:** Rosling-style narrated animation is an anecdotal counterexample the authors note; a talk with a live presenter differs from a page.

### F3. The zero-baseline rule is context-dependent, but truncation reliably inflates perceived effect size
- **Rule:** Bars: keep zero (the length encodes magnitude). For dots/lines a non-zero axis is allowed when the meaningful variation is far from zero, but truncation exaggerates perceived effect in line charts too, so set the range to the effect size you mean to show, state it in text, and do not rely on a broken-axis glyph to correct the reader. For effect-size communication one published heuristic is mean ±0.75 SD (Witt 2019).
- **Source:** Franconeri et al. 2021 (full text) + Correll, Bertini, Franconeri (2020), "Truncating the Y-Axis: Threat or Menace?" CHI 2020, doi:10.1145/3313831.3376222, arXiv:1907.02035 (full text downloaded; see F-contested C2).
- **Design/effect:** Correll et al. (crowdsourced; Exp. 1 N=40): truncation raised perceived effect severity, with "no robust difference" between bar and line charts; the effect persisted "even for viewers that are aware of the presence of truncated axes"; they "advise against ironclad rules." Franconeri summarises Pandey et al. 2015: perceived-contrast ratings averaged about 1.5 (zero baseline) vs 2.8 (deceptive baseline) on a 1-5 scale; Correll et al. 2020 found the exaggeration persisted even when participants typed the values first and a broken-axis symbol was shown.
- **Figure decision:** Supports /figure's "bars start at zero (else use dots)" but refines it: truncation is a *choice that sets perceived effect size*, so the axis range must match the size of effect the claim asserts. Keep the honesty rule; add "axis range is an effect-size claim."
- **Limits:** Witt's 0.75 SD rule targets standardized-effect reading; it is one proposal, not a consensus.

### F4. Annotate heavily; place each kind of text where it belongs; keep a stand-alone text version
- **Rule:** Default to a chart *with* several short annotations rather than a bare chart or a paragraph alone. Place text by what it says: encoding/axis explanations by the axis; statistics and specific values at the data point (mark the point); the trend/pattern claim in the title; external context at the relevant point. Always be able to produce a text-only version that stands alone.
- **Source:** Stokes, Setlur, Cogley, Satyanarayan, Hearst (2022), "Striking a Balance: Reader Takeaways and Preferences when Integrating Text and Charts," *IEEE TVCG* (VIS 2022), doi:10.1109/TVCG.2022.3209383, arXiv:2208.01780. **Full text read** (results 4.1-4.3, guidelines 5.5, limitations). Companion: Stokes & Hearst (2022), "Why More Text is (Often) Better," arXiv:2209.10789 (**abstract + intro read**).
- **Design/effect:** 512 MTurk recruits, 302 after exclusions; univariate line charts ranked from no text to text-only paragraph, plus free-text takeaways. VISUAL-preference readers ranked the most-annotated chart best (mean rank 1.37) and the all-text and no-text variants worst (3.21, 3.23). 14% (42) ranked the text-only paragraph first. Placement: L1 (encoding) text was matched in takeaways 11.3x more often when by the axis than as title; L3 (trend) text was matched most when in the title (point placement 0.49x). Their Guideline 1: "Rather than aiming for maximally minimalist design, annotate charts with relevant text." Companion paper: readers "preferred to be informed, even when it sacrificed simplicity," as long as added text was not redundant.
- **Figure decision:** /figure's step 1 (claim -> title) matches L3-in-title. Add: numbers go at the point they describe, not in a caption; the "sentence" arm of the sentence/table/chart choice is legitimate for a real minority of readers, so a figure should ship with a sentence that states its claim alone.
- **Limits:** Only univariate synthetic line charts with fictional topics and "abrupt peaks"; preference and takeaway match, not accuracy. Authors note the all-text variant carried more L4 context, which may have favoured it.

### F5. The caption and the chart must emphasise the same feature, or the chart wins
- **Rule:** Check that the feature your title/caption names is the most visually prominent feature of the chart. If it is not, redesign the chart (zoom/crop to the interval, highlight, or switch to a form that makes that feature salient) rather than relying on the text to redirect the eye.
- **Source:** Kim, Setlur, Agrawala (2021), "Towards Understanding How Readers Integrate Charts and Captions: A Case Study with Line Charts," CHI 2021, doi:10.1145/3411764.3445443, arXiv:2101.08235. **Full text read** (abstract, design guidelines section).
- **Design/effect:** 219 participants marked prominent regions on 43 single-line charts; a second crowdsourced study with 2,168 participants reported takeaways from chart-caption pairs. When chart and caption both emphasise a high-prominence feature, readers take that as the takeaway; "when the caption describes a low-prominence chart feature, readers rely on the chart and report a higher-prominence feature as the takeaway." External context in the caption further helps convey the caption's message.
- **Figure decision:** Gives the blind-reader test a concrete failure class to look for: a `claim` question answered with the chart's most salient feature instead of the titled one = caption/chart mismatch. The fix is in the chart, not the caption.
- **Limits:** Single-line charts only; template captions.

### F6. Titles steer what readers remember, and readers still think the chart is neutral
- **Rule:** Treat the title as the strongest lever on the takeaway. Write it as the finding (as /figure already says), but check it against the whole chart: a title that picks one of two visible stories will be remembered over the data, and most readers will not flag it. When the data carry two defensible readings, say so in the title or show both.
- **Source:** Kong, Liu, Karahalios (2018), "Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics," CHI 2018, doi:10.1145/3173574.3174012 (**abstract + first pages read**, via author PDF text); Kong, Liu, Karahalios (2019), "Trust and Recall of Information across Varying Degrees of Title-Visualization Misalignment," CHI 2019, doi:10.1145/3290605.3300576 (**abstract + author blog summary only**).
- **Design/effect:** 2018: same chart (e.g. U.S. defense budget as %GDP vs constant dollars) with opposite slanted titles; "the slant of the title influenced the perceived main message," viewers derived "opposing messages from the same visualization"; no significant attitude change. 2019: with miscued or contradictory titles, "visualizations were persistently perceived as impartial by the majority" and recall "more frequently aligned with the titles than the visualization." Sample sizes not verified (full text not read).
- **Figure decision:** Strengthens the claim-as-title rule and adds an honesty check: the blind reader should be given the chart *without* the title at least once, and its takeaway compared with the title.
- **Limits:** Political/controversial topics; effect on neutral technical figures is untested here.

### F7. Encode the claim's key comparison as position on a common scale; treat the ranking as a population average that depends on the task
- **Rule:** For the comparison that carries the claim, use position on a shared axis (dots, aligned bars). Accept length, angle or area for secondary information. Do not reject a familiar form (pie, table) on ranking alone when the reader's task is retrieve/extremum/cluster or part-to-whole with few slices.
- **Sources:** Cleveland & McGill (1984), "Graphical Perception...," *JASA* 79(387):531-554, doi:10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080 (**not read; known through the replications below**). Heer & Bostock (2010), "Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception," CHI 2010, doi:10.1145/1753326.1753357 (**full text read**, Exp. 1A/1B/2). Davis et al. (2022), "The Risks of Ranking," *IEEE TVCG*, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2022.3226463, arXiv:2212.10533 (**abstract + findings read**). Saket, Endert, Demiralp (2018), "Task-Based Effectiveness of Basic Visualizations," *IEEE TVCG*, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2018.2829750, arXiv:1709.08546 (**abstract, results summary, discussion read**).
- **Design/effect:** Heer & Bostock re-ran Cleveland & McGill's proportion-judgment task on MTurk (log2(|error|+1/8) measure): "the ranking of types by accuracy is consistent between the two experiments"; position still "significantly outperformed length"; circular area was worse than angle, both worse than position; angle was *not* worse than length, contrary to theory. Davis et al. (Bayesian multilevel re-analysis of a C&M replication): bars best on average, but "around 20-25% of people consistently perform better with another chart type" and "less than 40%" share the canonical bar > pie order. Saket et al. (180 crowd participants, 5 forms x 10 tasks): effectiveness "significantly varies across task and data attribute types"; tables, bars and pies were fastest and most accurate for Retrieve; pies were comparable to bars and tables on several tasks but poor for correlation and distribution; scatterplots never worst.
- **Figure decision:** /figure's form table is task-keyed already; add the reader-task tag (lookup/shape/claim) as the tiebreaker between forms, and make "position on common scale" the rule only for the claim's own comparison.
- **Limits:** Proportion-judgment tasks on abstract stimuli; decoding precision is not comprehension of a message (see Bertini/Correll/Franconeri 2020 in Contested).

### F8. Pictographs and embellishment that *are* the data do no harm and help memory; decoration unrelated to data distracts
- **Rule:** Minimalism is not a correctness rule. Allowed: icon arrays/pictographs that encode the counts, a clear finding-title and explanatory text (these raise recall). Not allowed: backgrounds or images unrelated to the data. Use icon arrays with a shared denominator for risk to lay readers.
- **Sources:** Bateman et al. (2010), "Useful Junk?," CHI 2010, doi:10.1145/1753326.1753716 (**full text read**). Borkin et al. (2016), "Beyond Memorability," *IEEE TVCG* 22(1):519-528, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2015.2467732 (**abstract + intro read**, via author PDF text). Borkin et al. (2013), "What Makes a Visualization Memorable?," doi:10.1109/TVCG.2013.234 (**not read**; claim taken from Franconeri 2021's summary). Haroz, Kosara, Franconeri (2015), "ISOTYPE Visualization," CHI 2015, doi:10.1145/2702123.2702275 (**abstract read**). Franconeri et al. 2021 (full text).
- **Design/effect:** Bateman: 20 participants, 14 Holmes-style vs plain charts; description accuracy "no worse," recall after 2-3 weeks "significantly better" for embellished. Borkin 2016: 393 labelled visualizations, 33 eye-tracked participants; supports that "titles and supporting text should convey the message," pictograms used appropriately "do not interfere with understanding and can improve recognition," and "redundancy helps." Haroz: "superfluous images can distract," but "no user costs - and some intriguing benefits - when pictographs are used to represent the data." Franconeri's summary: embellishments "can distract if unrelated to the data, but if they are related, they can improve viewers' memory and engagement"; pictorial elements improve memory that a chart was seen (Borkin 2013), with mixed evidence for memory of the data.
- **Figure decision:** Drop any /figure rule that implies "maximise data-ink." Keep "no decoration unrelated to data." For reports meant to be remembered (briefs, decks), a finding-title and a redundant text statement of the claim are the memorability levers, not images.
- **Limits:** Bateman N=20 with Holmes charts (strong, professionally drawn images); memorability studies measure recognition, which is not accuracy.

### F9. Show uncertainty as outcomes (quantile dotplots, HOPs), not bare error bars, for non-expert readers; say which interval it is
- **Rule:** For a lay or mixed audience: show a distribution of outcomes (quantile dotplot of ~20-100 dots; HOPs if the medium can animate; icon arrays for risks). If you use intervals, label them (SD, SE, 95% CI, predictive interval), and when the reader's question is "how much will this help a case," show the *predictive* interval, not only the CI of the mean. Avoid bar + error whisker; use point + interval or violin/gradient.
- **Sources:** Hullman, Resnick, Adar (2015), "Hypothetical Outcome Plots Outperform Error Bars and Violin Plots...," *PLOS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0142444 (**abstract + methods skim**). Kay, Kola, Hullman, Munson (2016), "When (ish) is My Bus?," CHI 2016, doi:10.1145/2858036.2858558 (**abstract read**). Correll & Gleicher (2014), "Error Bars Considered Harmful," *IEEE TVCG* (2014) (**abstract + design section read**). Hofman, Goldstein, Hullman (2020), CHI 2020, doi:10.1145/3313831.3376454 (**not read; via Franconeri 2021 full text**). Kale et al. (2019) HOPs-trends, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2018.2864909 (**not read**, fetch failed). Padilla/Kay/Hullman uncertainty review (**not located**).
- **Design/effect:** HOPs (MTurk): "With HOPs, people made much more accurate judgments about plots of two and three quantities"; accuracy similar across representations for single-quantity questions. Quantile dotplots: "reduce the variance of probabilistic estimates by ~1.15 times compared to density plots" and support more confident estimation. Correll & Gleicher: bar + error bar induces "within-the-bar bias" (values inside the bar judged likelier); gradient and violin plots produced judgments "more consistently aligned with statistical inferences." Franconeri (on Hofman 2020): lay viewers shown 95% CIs overestimated effect size and would pay more for a treatment than viewers shown predictive intervals; rescaling the axis to the predictive range helped only slightly.
- **Figure decision:** Tightens /figure's "estimate with uncertainty" row: add quantile dotplot as the default for single-forecast communication, and a rule to name the interval type. HOPs is the one legitimate use of animation (F2).
- **Limits:** Crowdworker studies with simple stimuli; expert readers (e.g. forest plots in papers) are a different population, and conventions matter there.

### F10. Bank slopes you want compared toward 45°, and choose the scale of the trend first
- **Rule:** When the claim is about the rate of change, set the aspect ratio so the relevant segments average about 45°. If the series has trends at several frequencies (seasonal + long-run), make one panel per scale, each banked for its own trend.
- **Sources:** Heer & Agrawala (2006), "Multi-Scale Banking to 45 Degrees," *IEEE TVCG* 12(5), doi:10.1109/TVCG.2006.163 (**full text skimmed**). Cleveland et al. (1988) slope-judgment experiments (**not read**; described in Heer & Agrawala).
- **Design/effect:** Heer & Agrawala is a technique paper, not a user study; it rests on Cleveland et al.'s experiments that viewers judge the ratio of two slopes "most accurately when the orientation resolution between them is maximized," which for two segments occurs around 45°. Multi-scale banking uses spectral analysis to find trends at different frequencies and produces a banked chart for each.
- **Figure decision:** Already in /figure ("aspect ratio banks the slopes you care about near 45°"); add "one panel per trend scale" for series with seasonal + long-run structure.
- **Limits:** Evidence is for slope-*ratio* judgments; Franconeri 2021 cites Ceja et al. (2021) "Truth or square" that aspect ratio also biases recall of positions (**not read**). Banking can conflict with the shared-axis rule across panels.

### F11. Design from the reader's task downward, and match the genre to how much the author should drive
- **Rule:** Before choosing the form, write the reader's task in abstract terms (lookup a value, compare two items, find extremes, see a trend, judge a distribution), then choose the encoding. An error at the task level cannot be repaired by encoding or styling. For explanatory pieces, choose an author-driven layout (annotated chart, slideshow/stepper) over free exploration.
- **Sources:** Munzner (2009), "A Nested Model for Visualization Design and Validation," *IEEE TVCG*, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2009.111 (**abstract read**). Segel & Heer (2010), "Narrative Visualization: Telling Stories with Data," *IEEE TVCG*, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2010.179 (**abstract read**).
- **Design/effect:** Both are frameworks, not experiments. Munzner: four nested levels (domain task/data -> abstract operations/data types -> encoding/interaction -> algorithm); "an upstream error inevitably cascades to all downstream levels." Segel & Heer: genres of narrative visualization characterised by "the balance between the narrative flow intended by the author ... and story discovery on the part of the reader."
- **Figure decision:** /figure's `claim/lookup/shape` tags are a compact Munzner task abstraction; keep them and make them the input to form choice. Segel & Heer's author-driven end matches the /figure default of claim-titled, annotated figures.
- **Limits:** Frameworks give process, not effect sizes.

### F12. Tables beat charts for exact retrieval and for readers who need values; visual encodings inside tables help comparisons
- **Rule:** When the reader's questions are lookups of a few values, use a table. When they must compare across many cells, add a visual encoding inside the table (shading, dots, bars in cells) instead of switching to a chart.
- **Sources:** Saket et al. 2018 (above, full results read); Franconeri et al. 2021 (full text, citing Price et al. 2016).
- **Design/effect:** Saket: tables were among the fastest and most accurate for Retrieve, but "relatively slow" for Order, Extremum and Distribution. Franconeri: older adults comparing health plans made "better and, in some cases, faster decisions" with a table carrying visual categorical encodings than with a text-and-numbers table (Price et al. 2016).
- **Figure decision:** Supports /figure's table row and its "table-heatmap" for the multiverse; generalise the heatmap idea to any comparison-heavy table.
- **Limits:** Small datasets; Price et al. not read directly.

### F13. You cannot judge your own figure's salience; test with a naive reader
- **Rule:** Never decide that "the pattern is obvious" from your own view. Pre-register what a reader should see, then have a reader who has not seen the analysis state what stands out. The blind reader must not receive the backstory.
- **Source:** Xiong, Van Weelden, Franconeri (2020), "The Curse of Knowledge in Visual Data Communication," *IEEE TVCG* 26(10):3051-3062, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2019.2917689 (**abstract read; figure and result via Franconeri 2021 full text**).
- **Design/effect:** Participants heard one of several backstories about polling data, then predicted what naive viewers would find most salient. They "were strongly influenced by their own knowledge, despite explicit instructions to ignore it."
- **Figure decision:** Direct empirical backing for /figure step 5. Implementation note: the blind-reader prompt must contain no analysis context, and the `claim` question should be open-ended ("what is the main point?") before targeted questions, so the reader's salience is measured before being cued.
- **Limits:** Humans with line charts; an LLM reader is a proxy whose salience may differ from a human's.

## Contested rules (disconfirmation pass)

| Famous rule | What the evidence says | Status for /figure |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximise data-ink / declutter** (Tufte 1983) | Franconeri 2021 (full text): "only mixed evidence that this practice improves aesthetic ratings and little evidence that the prescription affects objective performance"; Ajani et al. (2021) preferred decluttered versions, Inbar et al. 2007 / Hill et al. 2017 found the opposite; Gillan & Richman 1994 found removing some elements sped mean-judgments and removing others slowed them. Stokes et al. 2022 (full text): heavily annotated charts "were not penalized" for clutter. Ajani 2021 "discouraged further empirical testing given the small and mixed effects" (via Franconeri; Ajani not read). | Drop as a correctness rule. Keep "remove what distracts from the one comparison." |
| **Chartjunk always hurts** | Bateman 2010 (N=20): no accuracy cost, better 2-3-week recall. Haroz 2015: data-encoding pictographs cost nothing; superfluous images distract. Borkin 2016: pictograms "if used appropriately" do not interfere and aid recognition. Franconeri lists null results too (Helgeson & Moriarty 1993; Kelly 1989) and mixed ones (Li & Moacdieh 2014). | Split rule: data-related embellishment OK; unrelated imagery out. Bateman is small-N; do not generalise to "embellish." |
| **Pie charts are always worse** | Heer & Bostock 2010: angle judgments *not* worse than length (contrary to theory), area worse than both. Saket 2018: pies match bars/tables on retrieve, range, extremum, cluster; poor on correlation and distribution. Kosara & Skau 2016 (EuroVis short, abstract read): "no clear evidence" pies are read by central angle, "area and arc length appear to be at least equally important"; exploded, elliptical and square variants all raise error over a plain pie. Davis 2022: <40% of individuals share the bar > pie canonical order. | Allow a plain 2-5-slice pie for part-to-whole of one whole; never for comparing across pies, distribution or trend. No pie variants. |
| **Always start the y-axis at zero** | Correll, Bertini, Franconeri 2020 (full text): truncation inflates perceived effect in bars AND lines, persists when the viewer notices it; they reject ironclad rules and say to scale to the "meaningful effect sizes." Franconeri gives a counterexample: a 0-°F baseline hides warming. | Bars: zero. Dots/lines: range chosen to match the claimed effect size, stated in text. |
| **Rainbow colour maps are harmful** | Franconeri 2021 (full text): spectrum hues "exaggerate differences that happen to straddle the hue boundaries." Reda & Szafir (2020), "Rainbows Revisited," doi:10.1109/TVCG.2020.3030439 (**not read**; PDF download returned a wrong file) is the main counter-study, reported to find some rainbow designs perform acceptably for inference. | Owned by /dataviz. Flag only: the blanket ban is contested, the hue-boundary distortion is not. |
| **Cleveland-McGill ranking is universal** | Replicated at population level (Heer & Bostock 2010). Davis 2022: 20-25% of people consistently do better with a non-bar chart. Bertini, Correll, Franconeri (2020), "Why Shouldn't All Charts Be Scatter Plots?" (VIS short, intro read): precision-of-decoding is one goal; exemplary visualizations are "diverse, complex, and reward contemplation." | Use for the claim's own comparison only; not as a form ranking. |
| **Animation helps show change** | Franconeri 2021: "little evidence that animation facilitates understanding," plus an illusion-of-understanding effect; exception is sampling uncertainty (HOPs). | Static small multiples by default. |
| **Text is neutral decoration** | Kong 2018/2019: title slant sets recalled message; majority still rate chart impartial. Stokes et al. (2023), "The Role of Text in Visualizations," arXiv:2401.04052 (**abstract read**): added text "had a minimal effect on how people perceive data trends" in a prediction task but changed how biased readers judged the authors. | Text steers takeaways and trust more than it steers perception of the data itself. Write titles as findings, and check them against the chart. |

Two older "rules" were not checked against primary sources in this lane: the Tal & Wansink "trivial graphs increase persuasion" claim and its failed replication (Dragicevic & Jansen 2018, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2017.2744298; PDF fetch failed, **not read**), and Kale et al. 2019 HOPs for trends (**not read**).

## Implications for /figure (ranked)

1. Step 1: after the claim, name the ONE comparison and plan its grouping, highlight and annotation (F1, F5).
2. Step 2: the text-only arm is legitimate; ship every figure with a stand-alone claim sentence (F4, 14% text-preferring readers).
3. Step 3: add quantile dotplot + "name the interval" to the uncertainty row; allow plain pies for few-slice part-to-whole; replace "bars start at zero" wording with the effect-size framing (F3, F9, Contested).
4. Step 5: blind reader gets no backstory; ask an open "main point?" first; run once without the title to detect title/chart mismatch (F5, F6, F13).
5. Remove or avoid any "maximise data-ink" language; keep "nothing unrelated to the data" (F8, Contested).
