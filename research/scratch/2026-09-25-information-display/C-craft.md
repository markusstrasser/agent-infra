# Lane C: craft and artistic tradition of information display

**Verdict:** Across the canon, the newsroom and the artists, the moves that survive scrutiny are about *editing for one comparison*, not about minimalism or ornament. The recurring moves are: write the finding in the title, annotate the few points that carry it, label directly, grey the context, put comparisons within one eyespan, pick the scale and alignment that make the claim a straight line, and show the method and its flaws next to the graphic. Each works for one kind of task. Minimalism loses on recall and on reading novel forms, ornament loses on precise reading, and a beautiful chart can still mislead through a halo effect. So the /figure skill should pick moves by task (lookup vs claim vs recall vs exploration), not by an aesthetic creed.

Scope: craft moves from Tufte and Bertin, historical exemplars, newsroom practice (NYT, FT, Datawrapper, The Pudding), data-humanist designers and critics. Palettes, mark specs and tooltips are skipped because /dataviz owns them. Quotes are verbatim from sources I read unless marked *(secondary)*. Every URL below was returned by a search or fetch in this session. "When it fails" draws on cited evidence where it exists; my own inferences are labelled *(inference)*.

## Signature moves

### 1. Title the finding, then annotate the 2-3 points that carry it
- **Instruction:** Write the headline as the claim ("X doubled after Y"), not the topic. Add short text annotations on the specific marks that prove it, including one line saying why an outlier looks the way it does.
- **Who / exemplar:** Amanda Cox (NYT); John Burn-Murdoch (FT); Lisa Charlotte Muth (Datawrapper). Exemplar: the FT Covid trajectory tracker, which was "a static image each day with a lot of annotations" (Eye Magazine interview, https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch). Explainer: https://www.ft.com/video/9a72a9d4-8db1-4615-8333-4b73ae3ddff8
- **Why:** Cox: "If you annotate three points in a scatterplot, I'm probably good, even if I'm not super comfortable reading scatterplots. I also think the words in a graphic should highlight the relevant pattern, or an expert's interpretation, and not merely say 'Here is some data.' The annotation layer is critical" (https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/). Burn-Murdoch: "If you have a title which says, 'Number of Covid cases by day' people will understand the chart but they won't be prompted to do anything. Whereas if you give it a title that makes a point, you're going to get a stronger reaction" (Eye). The widely repeated Cox line "The annotation layer is the most important thing we do" reaches me only via Andy Kirk's book, citing Eyeo. I did not verify it at a primary source.
- **When it fails:** When the point-making title claims more than the data supports. Healy's worked example is an NYT small multiple that is "impressively well-produced" but plotted only the share of respondents answering 10/10. His warning: "the 'halo effect' accompanying a well-produced figure might make it _easier_ to mislead some audiences" (https://socviz.co/01-look-at-data.html). The move also fails for exploratory essays, where the author deliberately withholds a thesis (see disagreement B).

### 2. Label marks directly and remove the legend when you can
- **Instruction:** Put each series name at the end of its line or beside its cluster, in the series colour. Remove the colour key. Repeat units where they are read.
- **Who / exemplar:** Muth/Datawrapper; Burn-Murdoch; Rosling. Exemplar: the Datawrapper sea-ice chart praised in "Respect your readers' time" (https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/readers-time). Rosling TED 2006 also places labels beside the bubbles (https://www.ted.com/talks/hans_rosling_the_best_stats_you_ve_ever_seen).
- **Why:** Muth: "Place the words that explain your chart elements as close to those elements as possible." "Imagine if every object in a museum were labeled only right next to the door." "Don't make your readers eye-travel that much." (https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)
- **When it fails:** On narrow or mobile widths and with many series. Datawrapper's own behaviour turns direct labels back into a key "once the chart width is too narrow" (https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/line-charts). For unfamiliar encodings the legend itself is the teaching device. Fragapane: "the legend is the bridge between me and and the readers [sic]... I really spend a lot of time, designing the legends" (https://policyviz.com/podcast/episode-266-federica-fragapane/).

### 3. Grey the context and colour only what the claim is about, ranked by priority
- **Instruction:** Draw every comparison series in light grey. Give the focal series one strong colour, and at most one mid-strength colour for the reference group. Use the smallest contrast that still separates each layer.
- **Who / exemplar:** Burn-Murdoch (FT trajectory and excess-deaths charts); Datawrapper; Tufte. Exemplar: the Datawrapper sea-ice chart, where "The boldest color (black) for the current year; the 2nd most striking color (blue) for the last ten years; the rest in grey. The priorities are exactly what they should be." (Muth, https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/readers-time)
- **Why:** Tufte, Visual Explanations p.73: "Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective." I have this wording from several transcriptions *(secondary)*; I did not read the book directly. On layering, Tufte in Envisioning Information: "Among the most powerful devices for reducing noise and enriching the content of displays is the technique of layering and separation, visually stratifying various aspects of the data" (EI ch.3 PDF, https://ocw.ui.ac.id/pluginfile.php/1323/mod_folder/content/0/37-65_EDWARD%20TUFTE__Envisioning_Information.pdf).
- **When it fails:** When readers care about a different item than the one you coloured. Burn-Murdoch notes people used his charts "to make points that I can't fathom how they decided that this chart makes that point" (https://voices.media/transcript-john-burn-murdoch-senior-data-visualisation-journalist-financial-times/). Offer a user-chosen highlight, or split into small multiples. *(inference)* Very subtle greys also vanish on projectors and in print.

### 4. Enforce comparison within one eyespan: small multiples with a constant design
- **Instruction:** Ask "compared with what?" and put both sides of the comparison on screen together. For more than 4-5 series, repeat one small panel per group, sorted meaningfully, with identical design across panels.
- **Who / exemplar:** Tufte; FT. Exemplar: Burn-Murdoch's FT excess-deaths small multiples, with a thick red current year over thin grey past years and the excess figure labelled in each panel. See the analysis at https://visualisations.substack.com/p/reading-between-the-lines *(secondary description of the FT graphic)*.
- **Why:** Tufte, EI p.67: "At the heart of quantitative reasoning is a single question: Compared to what? Small multiple designs, multivariate and data bountiful, answer directly by visually enforcing comparisons of changes, of the differences among objects, of the scope of alternatives." "Constancy of design puts the emphasis on changes in data, not changes in data frames." "Comparisons must be enforced within the scope of the eyespan, a fundamental point occasionally forgotten in practice." (EI ch.4 PDF, https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s12/files/2012/01/EnvisioningInformationChap4.pdf). Few gives the mechanism: small multiples "reduce cognitive load by placing everything that must be compared in front of the user's eyes at once" (https://perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/visual_difficulties.pdf).
- **When it fails:** Panels with independent y-scales make magnitudes look comparable when they are not. The FT graphic handled this by printing the excess number in each panel, because area between lines on different scales is hard to compare (Shashidhar, above). *(inference)* Too many panels shrink below legibility on mobile.

### 5. Choose the scale and alignment that turn the claim into a straight line, then teach the reader to read them
- **Instruction:** When the claim is about rates or ratios, use a log axis. Align series on a shared starting event ("days since 100th case") and add reference lines (doubling every 2/3/7 days). Say "log scale" in the subtitle, and consider a linear companion for general readers.
- **Who / exemplar:** Burn-Murdoch, FT Coronavirus trajectory tracker (explainer video above).
- **Why:** "Viruses spread exponentially... instead of a line that looks a bit like a hockey stick and shoots up into the sky, you get a nice straight line." On per-capita: "With this chart, we're focusing on trajectory." (FT video transcript). He also admits the choice is editorial: once changing these measures "will change the order in which countries appear... you're making an editorial decision" (voices.media transcript).
- **When it fails:** With general audiences. In a randomised experiment with about 2,000 US adults, the log-scale group had "a less accurate understanding of how the pandemic has developed, make less accurate predictions on its evolution, and have different policy preferences" (Romano et al. 2020, https://doi.org/10.1002/hec.4143, full text on PMC read). A Canadian study they cite found no effect. Defaults: a linear companion or explicit teaching annotations.

### 6. Ask the reader to predict before revealing
- **Instruction:** When the true pattern is surprising, first ask for a guess: let the reader draw the line, or write their expectation in text. Then overlay the real data on the guess and explain the gap.
- **Who / exemplar:** Quealy, Cox and Aisch, NYT "You Draw It: How Family Income Predicts Children's College Chances" (2015), https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/05/28/upshot/you-draw-it-how-family-income-affects-childrens-college-chances.html. Rosling uses a spoken version: he shows his students' predictions before running the animation (Dlugan analysis, https://sixminutes.dlugan.com/six-simple-techniques-for-presenting-data-hans-rosling-ted-2006/).
- **Why:** Aisch: "the surprising nature of this correlation is more compelling after one has been forced to think about it first" (https://driven-by-data.net/2015/05/28/college-chart-game). Evidence: participants "prompted to reflect on their prior knowledge by predicting and self-explaining data outperform a control group in recall and comprehension" (Kim, Reinecke & Hullman, CHI 2017, https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2017-ExplainingTheGap-CHI.pdf).
- **When it fails:** When the answer is unsurprising, since the interaction costs more than it returns (see move 7). It also fails when exact values matter: in the same paper, visual conditions had "36% more errors on average per recalled value... compared to text". Use a table or sentence for lookups.

### 7. Put everything important in the static view; make interaction earn its place
- **Instruction:** Assume the reader only scrolls. Never hide a key number in a tooltip or a later tab. Add interaction only when it delivers something a static view cannot, such as personal lookup, reweighting or prediction.
- **Who / exemplar:** Archie Tse (NYT), "Why We Are Doing Fewer Interactives," Malofiej 2016 slides, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf
- **Why:** "If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen." "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it. If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it." The result: "Most visuals are static." "We are writing and editing a lot more text."
- **When it fails:** For tools where the reader's own question is the point. Stefaner's OECD Better Life Index lets users weight 11 topics, and each petal "will become broader and more saturated" (https://truth-and-beauty.net/projects/oecd-better-life-index). Aisch's nuance: ignored tooltips don't mean "you shouldn't use any tooltips", only that you "should not hide important content behind interactions". I have this *(secondary)*, via a Columbia lecture PDF quoting Aisch 2017.

### 8. Show the detail so the overview emerges from it, and split the averages
- **Instruction:** Where readers will ask "which one?", plot individual units (dots, countries, quintiles) rather than a single aggregate. Let the pattern come from their mass. Break the aggregate open at the moment the claim depends on the variation inside it.
- **Who / exemplar:** Tufte (micro/macro readings); Rosling; John Snow. Exemplar: Rosling, TED 2006, who splits the Sub-Saharan Africa bubble into countries and Uganda into income quintiles.
- **Why:** Tufte, EI ch.2: "Simplicity of reading derives from the context of detailed and complex information, properly arranged. A most unconventional design strategy is revealed: to clarify, add detail." "CONFUSION and clutter are failures of design, not attributes of information." (EI PDF above). Rosling (transcript): "it's dangerous to use average data because there's such a lot of difference within countries"; and "the differences are much bigger than the weakness of the data."
- **When it fails:** When counts are shown without denominators. Tufte on Snow's dot map: it "does not fully answer the question Compared with what?... an area of the map may be free of cases merely because it is not populated" (Visual Explanations ch.2, https://library.stlawu.edu/system/files/2021-02/tufte-visual-and-statistical.pdf). Pair dots with a rate. *(inference)* It also fails through overplotting at large n; switch to density or hexbin, which the skill already covers.

### 9. Layer and separate: data forward, scaffolding back, and remove accidental patterns
- **Instruction:** Give data the darkest value and heaviest weight. Draw gridlines, axes and frames in light grey or remove them. Avoid boxes around words, parallel heavy lines and hatching. Each of these creates shapes that carry no information.
- **Who / exemplar:** Tufte (EI ch.3, after Josef Albers). Exemplar: the FT excess-deaths panels in move 4 (thin grey past years under a thick red 2020).
- **Why:** Tufte: "the various elements collected together on flatland interact, creating non-information patterns... Josef Albers described this visual effect as 1 + 1 = 3 or more." "Unless deliberate obscurity is sought, avoid surrounding words by little boxes." "Clarity is not everything, but there is little without it." (EI ch.3 PDF)
- **When it fails:** When subtraction goes past the reader's needs. Healy reports that in Anderson et al. 2011, "the minimalist version from Tufte's own work... proved to be the most cognitively difficult for viewers to interpret", and adds: "Cues like labels and gridlines... may often be an aid rather than an impediment" (socviz ch.1). Keep light gridlines when readers will read values off the chart.

### 10. Put word-sized graphics inside the sentence or table
- **Instruction:** When a trend supports a sentence, draw it at text height inline. Label its first, last, minimum and maximum values with numbers. Keep line weight matched to the surrounding type, and don't flatten the aspect ratio.
- **Who / exemplar:** Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice" (2004), https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/
- **Why:** "A sparkline is a small intense, simple, word-sized graphic with typographic resolution." "Data graphics should have the resolution of typography." "Sparklines often get their quantitative scaling context from nearby words, numbers and graphics" (history essay, https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparklines-history-by-tufte-1324-to-now/). This maps to Tufte's principle "Completely integrate words, numbers, images, diagrams" (Beautiful Evidence ch.5, *(secondary)* transcription).
- **When it fails:** Tufte's own complaint about implementations: "Need to be more thoughtful about aspect ratios (too many very flat sparklines in particular)" and "Keep the size and line weights consistent with the local typography" (https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/validation-of-sparkline-computer-code/). A sparkline without anchor numbers carries shape only. It cannot carry magnitude.

### 11. Keep the same marks across views; animate only to preserve identity
- **Instruction:** When one dataset is cut several ways, reuse the same marks and move them between arrangements, so each item can be followed. Keep positions stable between reloads. Cut or cross-fade when switching to an unrelated measure.
- **Who / exemplar:** Shan Carter, NYT "Four Ways to Slice Obama's 2013 Budget Proposal" (2012), https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/interactive/2012/02/13/us/politics/2013-budget-proposal-graphic.html. Its bubbles are sized by spending and coloured by cut or increase, regrouped as all, mandatory vs discretionary, changes, and departments. Mechanism: Mike Bostock, "Object Constancy," https://bost.ocks.org/mike/constancy/
- **Why:** Bostock: "Animated transitions are pretty, but they also serve a purpose: they make it easier to follow the data." "Above all, animation should be meaningful." "Transitions between unrelated datasets or dimensions... should use a simpler cross-fade or cut."
- **When it fails:** A contemporary critique of the budget piece says circle areas are "difficult to compare" and "every time I reload that view, the circles are in a new arrangement". It preferred the prior year's stable treemap (https://coffeespoons.me/2012/02/2013-budget-proposal/). Motion keeps identity but does not make area readable.

### 12. Match each visual variable to the level of the data
- **Instruction:** Encode quantities with position or length. Use lightness or size only for order. Use hue or shape only for categories. When you use area, the value must be proportional to area, not radius, and you accept lower precision.
- **Who / exemplar:** Jacques Bertin; Florence Nightingale's own correction. Her 1858 radius-scaled chart was redrawn so that the area is proportional to deaths (Friendly & Andrews 2021, https://doi.org/10.2436/20.8080.02.106).
- **Why:** Bertin: "The visual variables must have a level of organization at least equal to that of the components which they represent." "An order will not be perceptible if the variable is not ordered; a ratio will not be perceptible if the variable is not quantitative." (Semiology of Graphics, English edition PDF). Bertin held that quantitative perception is "restricted to location and size only" (Roth 2015 summary, https://geography.wisc.edu/cartography/projects/publications/Roth_2015_EG.pdf). On Nightingale's fix, Friendly & Andrews: "the eye tends to perceive the area rather than length in such displays".
- **When it fails:** Correct area encoding is still weak for exact comparison. Critics call the rose "a poor example of what we would like to see in clear and efficient data visualisation" (Ciecka, Significance 2024, https://doi.org/10.1093/jrssig/qmae066), and Kosara says it "hides the real pattern in a confusing representation" (https://kosara.net/papers/2016/Kosara-BELIV-2016.pdf). Use area only when the gestalt comparison is the point (move 13).

### 13. Frame the comparison that makes the argument, and say that it is a frame
- **Instruction:** Pick the split, alignment or comparison group that puts the claim in front of the reader: before vs after an intervention, the group vs familiar benchmarks. Then state the framing choice in a subtitle or note.
- **Who / exemplar:** Nightingale's two roses (Hugh Small's analysis, https://www.florence-nightingale-avenging-angel.co.uk/Nightingale_Hockey_Stick.pdf); W.E.B. Du Bois, "Illiteracy of the American Negroes compared with that of other nations" (1900), https://www.loc.gov/item/2014645352/, which sets Black American illiteracy beside eight European nations and Russia.
- **Why:** Small redrew Nightingale's rose as a column chart and found it "didn't convey the message that Nightingale wanted". She split the two years to show "before and after", and used area to "downplay month on month variation". Silas Munro on the Du Bois plates: "Du Bois and his team used information design as a rhetorical device" (via the Cartographic Perspectives review, https://doi.org/10.14714/cp93.1555, *(secondary)*).
- **When it fails:** Persuasive frames can mislead through what they juxtapose. Small, on Minard: the temperature strip "misleadingly suggests that Napoleon's army froze to death... most of the army was lost during the advance" (https://www.york.ac.uk/depts/maths/histstat/small.htm). Kosara says Snow's map was "created as a tool for persuasion, not to find the source of the disease" (BELIV 2016). Disclose the frame and, where you can, show the unframed version one click away.

### 14. Use a distinctive form or beauty to earn attention, but keep it subordinate to the reading
- **Instruction:** For general or ambient audiences (exhibits, covers, social media), a distinctive form, colour or metaphor is legitimate, provided it encodes the data or reinforces the message and does not hide values. Sketch the data's structure before opening a tool. Skip this move for analytic, lookup or agent audiences.
- **Who / exemplar:** Du Bois 1900 plates (above); Nadieh Bremer; Moritz Stefaner (OECD flowers, above); Giorgia Lupi. Fragapane, "The Stories Behind a Line" (http://www.storiesbehindaline.com/), draws one line per asylum seeker and reveals the map only "in a second moment".
- **Why:** The Du Bois exhibit sat "in the right corner of a room", so it had to "radiate its own sparkle and originality" (Aldon Morris, via the Cartographic Perspectives review). Bremer: "you first need to hook them in before you can try to convey the insights... The creativity, style and beauty should be the extra layer on top and shouldn't take it into a weird area where the design is bigger than the story" (https://builtin.com/data-science/data-sketches). Stefaner: "truth and beauty are equally important. In a visualization project, if you have only one without the other, you are not done yet" (https://medium.com/@moritz_stefaner/new-challenges-for-data-design-aefa11956b9d). Lupi: "I draw with data in my mind, but with no data in my pen" (http://giorgialupi.com/data-humanism-my-manifesto-for-a-new-data-wold). Evidence on memory: "color and the inclusion of a human recognizable object enhance memorability"; "common graphs are less memorable than unique visualization types" (Borkin et al. 2013, abstract only, PubMed 24051797).
- **When it fails:** Few restates what the embellishment evidence covers: "When a simple message—one that could be expressed verbally in a single sentence—is presented in a chart that includes embellishments relevant to that message". He also notes the study's plain controls "were in fact plain ugly" (https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/the_chartjunk_debate.pdf). Recall is not comprehension. Tufte: "If the numbers are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers" (EI p.34-35, quoted by Few). Fragapane chose plain lines for Stories Behind a Line because "such a complex topic deserves rationality and also a simple clearness".

### 15. Show the seams: method, sources and flaws next to the graphic
- **Instruction:** Put a short method and caveat line in the figure's notes layer. Name what is measured, as distinct from what the reader will assume, and link the data. Relabel axes when the measure is narrower than its name.
- **Who / exemplar:** The Pudding, "Film Dialogue from 2,000 screenplays, Broken Down by Gender and Age" (Anderson & Daniels), https://pudding.cool/2017/03/film-dialogue/, which says "This dataset isn't perfect", gives its methodology and links its sources and GitHub data. Burn-Murdoch relabelled the FT axis from "cumulative number of cases" to "confirmed positive tests" (FT video).
- **Why:** Tufte's principle "Thoroughly describe the evidence" (Beautiful Evidence ch.5, *(secondary)*). On Snow: "Detailed comments on possible errors annotate both the map and the table, reassuring readers about the care and integrity" (Visual Explanations ch.2 PDF). Lupi: "'Data-driven' doesn't mean 'unmistakably true,' and it never did."
- **When it fails:** When caveats compete with the claim for attention. Keep them in the notes layer (move 9), not the title. Healy's democracy example shows that a missing measurement note can invert the reading (move 1).

## Taste disagreements

**A. Minimalism vs embellishment.** Tufte holds that chartjunk carries "contempt both for the information and for the audience" and that "Credibility vanishes in clouds of chartjunk" (EI, via Few). Against him: Holmes, Bateman et al. 2010 (embellished charts recalled better after 2-3 weeks, with no comprehension loss) and Borkin 2013 (unique forms are more memorable). Healy adds that Tufte's minimal boxplot was the hardest to read. The middle ground: Kosara redefines junk as "any element of a chart that does not contribute to clarifying the intended message" (https://eagereyes.org/blog/2013/definition-chart-junk), and Few says "nothing that supports the chart's message in a meaningful way is junk". The split that resolves most of it is task: analysis and lookup favour lean designs, while presentation and recall tolerate relevant embellishment. Kosara: the canon charts "were made for presentation rather than analysis" (BELIV 2016).

**B. State the thesis, or depict the system.** Cox and Burn-Murdoch want a title that makes a point. Matt Daniels (The Pudding) deliberately does not: "depict the system... rather than a thesis/point"; readers "would much rather form their own judgements" (https://medium.com/@matthew_daniels/the-journalist-engineer-c9c1a72b993f). Lupi: "clarity does not need to come all at once". This bears directly on /figure step 1. A claim-first title fits presentation figures; an exploratory essay may legitimately withhold one and guide with prose instead.

**C. Glanceable vs slow reading.** The newsroom optimises for a scroll: "Readers just want to scroll" (Tse). Lupi ("impractical to avoid a certain level of visual complexity") and Fragapane ("the visual equivalent of a long article and they need time to be read", https://nightingaledvs.com/can-data-visualization-help-us-talk-to-children-about-earth/) design for dwell time. Tufte sits closer to the artists on density ("to clarify, add detail") than his minimalist reputation suggests.

**D. Log vs linear.** Burn-Murdoch's log scale is right for the growth-rate question. Romano et al. show that the general public misreads it. Both hold, for different audiences.

**E. Legend: delete it or design it.** Muth says remove the key and label directly. Fragapane calls the legend "the bridge" and designs it carefully. For standard forms, go with Muth. For novel forms, the legend is necessary.

**F. Are the canon exemplars good models?** Tufte calls Minard's march possibly "the best statistical graphic ever drawn" (via Healy). Small says Minard's temperature strip misleads. Kosara says Snow's map was persuasion and Nightingale's hides the pattern. Small and Friendly defend the rose as fit for its before/after argument. The lesson to carry forward is the rhetorical fit between form and argument, not the form itself.

**G. Beauty as a truth signal.** Stefaner: if you have truth without beauty, "you are not done yet". Healy: good aesthetics can create a halo that makes misleading easier, and "does not make it much harder for you to mislead yourself".

## What this suggests for /figure (inference, for the parent to judge)
- Step 1: allow an explicit exploratory branch that depicts the system without a thesis (Daniels, Lupi), rather than treating a missing claim only as a sign of not-yet-knowing.
- Step 2/3: add a task axis (lookup / claim / recall / explore). Several moves flip sign across it: minimalism, embellishment, log scale, interaction.
- New checks for step 5 (render and look): is the finding in the title; are there 2-3 annotations; are labels direct; is context grey with one focal colour; are compared items in one eyespan; is the scale stated; is there a notes line on what is measured.
- The blind reader could test the halo effect: add a `claim` question whose correct answer contradicts the figure's visual impression. This is a gap; no source proposes it.
