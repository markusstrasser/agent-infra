## 41 — Scenario-as-baseline

### C-41.1 RCP8.5 adopted as "business as usual" — GROUNDED (grade A), lead partly CONTRADICTED
**Date:** scenario published 2011; correction 2020-01-29; institutional adoption of correction 2022.
**Number as used:** "nearly 5 °C of warming by the end of the century" presented as the no-policy / business-as-usual
trajectory, and used as the high arm in impact studies, IPCC AR5 and the 2019 Special Report on the Ocean and
Cryosphere, and in downstream cost-of-climate and risk-disclosure work.
**Primary:** Hausfather Z. & Peters G.P., "Emissions – the 'business as usual' story is misleading", *Nature*
577, 618-620, 29 Jan 2020. DOI **10.1038/d41586-020-00177-3**. Read 2026-09-16 (full text via Exa publication
record + verbatim reproduction at regulatorwatch.com/reported_elsewhere/emissions-the-business-as-usual-story-is-misleading/).
> "RCP8.5 was intended to explore an unlikely high-risk future. But it has been widely used by some experts,
> policymakers and the media as something else entirely: as a likely 'business as usual' outcome. A sizeable
> portion of the literature on climate impacts refers to RCP8.5 as business as usual, implying that it is
> probable in the absence of stringent climate mitigation."

> "Emission pathways to get to RCP8.5 generally require an unprecedented fivefold increase in coal use by the
> end of the century, an amount larger than some estimates of recoverable coal reserves."

> "Assessment of current policies suggests that the world is on course for around 3 °C of warming above
> pre-industrial levels by the end of the century — still a catastrophic outcome, but a long way from 5 °C."

**Measured magnitude:** baseline-choice gap = **~5 °C (RCP8.5) vs ~3 °C (current policy)**, i.e. the scenario
adopted as BAU overstates end-century warming by roughly **2 °C**; the driving input assumption is a **5x**
increase in coal use (Ritchie & Dowlatabadi 2017, cited as ref. 6, put the implied figure at ~6x current coal).
**Institutional correction:** IPCC AR6 WG3 Ch.3 (Riahi et al. 2022, p. 386): "High-end scenarios (like RCP-8.5)
can be very useful to explore high-end risks of climate change but are not typical 'business-as-usual'
projections and should therefore not be presented as such." — grade A, quoted via uni-hamburg Climate Matters
2022-07-11 (page cite given; IPCC PDF not opened this epoch).
**CONTRADICTION of the lead:** the lead asserted the comment contains "the count of papers using it as BAU and
the IPCC/IEA emissions comparison." The primary contains **no paper count** — the quantifier is the qualitative
"a sizeable portion of the literature". Do **not** train on a numeric paper count attributed to this comment.
The IEA comparison is present only as a reference (IEA *World Energy Outlook 2019*, ref. 7) supporting the ~3 °C
current-policy figure, not as a tabulated emissions comparison in the text.
**Also CONTRADICTED:** the lead's implied article id `d41586-020-00177-4` 404s. Correct suffix is **-3**.
**Counter-direction inside the same dispute:** Schwalm, Glendon & Duffy (PNAS 2020) argue RCP8.5 best tracks
*cumulative* historical emissions and remains the appropriate near-term reference; Hausfather & Peters state
the opposing read in the same comment ("reports of emissions over the past decade suggest that they are
actually closer to those in the median scenarios"). The disagreement is about which construction is the
baseline, which is exactly mechanism 41.
**Direction:** the BAU framing served climate-urgency advocacy; the correction was authored by climate
scientists (Breakthrough Institute / CICERO), not by opponents of mitigation — so this is an **intra-coalition**
construction dispute, not a left/right split. Hausfather & Peters state the political cost they feared:
"Overstating the likelihood of extreme climate impacts can make mitigation seem harder than it actually is."

## Relative-vs-absolute (checklist 8-adjacent)

### C-08.1 Pfizer/BioNTech "95% effective" (RRR) vs ~0.84 pp absolute risk reduction — GROUNDED (grade A)
**Date:** NEJM publication 2020-12-10; the 95% figure carried the US/EU authorization communications and every
subsequent mandate debate through 2021-2022.
**Primary:** Polack F.P. et al., "Safety and Efficacy of the BNT162b2 mRNA Covid-19 Vaccine", *N Engl J Med*
2020;383:2603-2615, read 2026-09-16 via PMC7745181.
> "Among 36,523 participants who had no evidence of existing or prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, 8 cases of Covid-19
> with onset at least 7 days after the second dose were observed among vaccine recipients and 162 among
> placebo recipients."

> "BNT162b2 was 95% effective in preventing Covid-19 (95% credible interval, 90.3 to 97.6)."

Denominators (Table 2): BNT162b2 **N = 18,198**; placebo **N = 18,325**.
**The paper reports no absolute risk reduction anywhere.** (Verified against the fetched full text.)
**Recomputation from the primary table (this memo's arithmetic):**

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| placebo attack rate | 162 / 18,325 = **0.8841 %** |
| vaccine attack rate | 8 / 18,198 = **0.04396 %** |
| relative risk reduction (the headline) | 1 − (0.04396 / 0.8841) = **95.03 %** |
| absolute risk reduction over the ~2-month follow-up | 0.8841 − 0.04396 = **0.8402 pp** |
| number needed to vaccinate | 1 / 0.008402 = **119** |

**Construction point:** both numbers are correct and describe the same two cells. RRR is scale-free and
transfers across incidence settings; ARR is bound to the trial's short follow-up and its low background
incidence, so it is *not* the lifetime or epidemic-wide absolute benefit either. Mechanism: a ledger that
publishes only the scale-free ratio, in a debate whose decision (mandate / individual uptake) turns on the
absolute quantity at the relevant incidence.
**Both directions, same paragraph:** mandate proponents and health agencies quoted "95% effective" without the
absolute complement; mandate opponents quoted "0.84% absolute" without stating that ARR scales with incidence
and that the trial's 2-month window suppresses it (at 10x background incidence the ARR is ~8 pp, NNV ~12).
Each side omitted the term that damages it. The *paper's* own omission is the ARR, and that omission is
grade-A verifiable.
**Direction:** the published construction served the pro-vaccination case; the omitted construction served the
skeptic case.

### C-46.1 "Firearms are the leading cause of death for children" — GROUNDED (grade A/B), ranking FLIPS on definition
**Date:** NEJM letter 2022-04-20; political uses 2022-05-25 and 2023-03-27.
**Political use:** Sen. Chuck Schumer, Senate floor, 2022-05-25: "The leading cause of death among children is a
firearm." Schumer's Director of Communications **named the NEJM letter as the office's source** (PolitiFact
2022-06-04) — an on-record citation chain from letter to floor speech. Barack Obama, 2023-03-27, after the
Covenant School shooting: "Guns are now the leading cause of death for children in the U.S."
**Primary construction:** Goldstick, Cunningham & Carter, "Current Causes of Death in Children and Adolescents
in the United States", *NEJM* 2022;386:1955-1956, defines the population as **"children and adolescents,
defined as persons 1 to 19 years of age"** and reports "From 2019 to 2020, the relative increase in the rate of
firearm-related deaths of all types (suicide, homicide, unintentional, and undetermined) among children and
adolescents was 29.5% — more than twice as high as the relative increase in the general population."
(nejm.org returned 403 to this agent on 2026-09-16; the age-range definition and the 29.5% sentence are quoted
verbatim by Snopes 2023-03-29 and PolitiFact 2022-06-04, both quoting the letter — grade drops to **B** for the
letter text itself, A for the CDC counts below.)
**Stated rationale for the lower bound** (Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions, *A Year in Review:
2020 Gun Deaths in the U.S.*): "infants (under age 1) are at a unique risk for age-specific causes of death,
including perinatal period deaths and congenital anomalies. In 2020, 11 infants were killed by firearms." This
is a published, epidemiologically defensible exclusion — record it as such.
**CDC WONDER 2020 counts as reported by the analyses (this memo's recomputation across age bands):**

| age band | firearm deaths | competing leader | firearm rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–19 | **4,357** | motor vehicle 4,112 | **1st** (+245, +6.0 %) |
| 1–18 | **3,219** | motor-vehicle traffic 2,882 | **1st** (+337, +11.7 %) |
| 1–17 | **2,270** | motor vehicle ~2,400 | **2nd** (−130, −5.4 %) — *ranking flips* |
| 0–17 | 2,270 + 11 infant = **2,281** | congenital anomalies 4,403 (then short gestation 3,141, SIDS 1,389) | **not 1st**; 2,281 = 51.8 % of the leader |

Sources for the counts: KFF analysis of CDC 2020 (1–19: 4,357 vs 4,112); PolitiFact citing CDC (1–18: 3,219 vs
2,882); NBC News analysis of CDC (1–17: ~2,400 MV vs 2,270 firearm); CDC 2020 infant causes (4,403 / 3,141 /
1,389 / 11). All are analyses *of* CDC WONDER rather than a WONDER extract pulled by this agent — grade **B**
for the individual cells, with an independent grade-A corroboration of the *rank*: The Trace's Gun Violence
Data Hub, which queried CDC WONDER directly in Nov 2025, states "In 2001, guns were the eighth leading cause of
death for Americans under 18. By 2023, they were fourth, outpacing car crashes and cancer," and that guns
"became the leading **injury** cause of death for Americans under 18 in 2020."
**Measured magnitude of the definitional choice:** moving the upper bound from 19 to 17 and restoring age 0
moves firearms from **rank 1 to rank 4** among under-18s. Two years at the top and one year at the bottom carry
the entire headline.
**Both directions, same paragraph:** the advocacy use is definitional expansion — "children" in ordinary usage
does not include 18- and 19-year-olds, and 82.6 % of 2021 pediatric firearm deaths were in the 15–19 band
(Roberts et al., *Pediatrics* 2023;152:e2023061296, CDC WONDER 2018-2021), so the pooled statistic is carried by
older teenagers; Veronica Pear (UC Davis) states the failure mode on the record: "someone could wrongly
interpret the statement to mean that firearms are the leading cause of death for each individual age within the
1 to 19 range … the very high rates among teens are swamping the very low rate among young kids." **And** the
counter-use is also a construction choice: capping at 17 while including infants imports perinatal and
congenital causes that no policy lever addresses and that the field routinely excludes for that reason, and the
narrower true claim (leading cause of *injury* death for under-18s, 2020-2023) survives every age cut tested
here. Both the expansion and the rebuttal move the boundary to get the rank they want.
**Direction:** 1–19 served gun-control advocacy; 0–17 served gun-rights rebuttal. Item 46, cleanly.

**Primary #1 — the publication page** (gov.uk/government/publications/hm-treasury-analysis-the-long-term-economic-impact-of-eu-membership-and-the-alternatives, HM Treasury Cm 9250, read 2026-09-16):

### C-46.2 "Mass shooting" counts — GROUNDED (grade A), 31.5x spread on the same year
**Political use:** running annual counts ("over 600 mass shootings this year") are the standard citation in
gun-control advocacy and executive statements; the FBI active-shooter count (dozens per year) is the standard
citation in rebuttal. Both sides quote a count that is correct under its own definition.
**Primary #1:** Booty M., O'Dwyer J., Webster D., McCourt A., Crifasi C., "Describing a 'mass shooting': the
role of databases in understanding burden", *Injury Epidemiology* 2019;6:47, DOI 10.1186/s40621-019-0226-7
(PMC6889601), read 2026-09-16. Same year (2017), four databases:
> "Gun Violence Archive recorded the most mass shooting incidents at **346** incidents in 2017, while Mother
> Jones only recorded **11** cases. Only **2** events were found in all four mass shooting datasets. When the
> strictest definition – four or more individuals fatally shot – was applied to all datasets, the number of
> mass shootings in 2017 ranged from **24** (Gun Violence Archive) to **5** (Mother Jones)."

**This memo's arithmetic:** raw spread **346 / 11 = 31.5x**. After harmonizing every dataset to the *same*
strictest definition the spread is still **24 / 5 = 4.8x** — so roughly a **6.6x** factor is definitional and a
residual **4.8x** is collection/inclusion, not definition. That decomposition is the useful number: harmonizing
the definition removes most, but nowhere near all, of the disagreement.
**Primary #2 (larger panel):** Bridges T., Tober T.L., Brazzell M., "Database discrepancies in understanding
the burden of mass shootings in the United States, 2013–2020", *Lancet Reg Health Am* 2023;22:100504, DOI
10.1016/j.lana.2023.100504 (PMC10192935):
> "The datasets collectively include **3155** incidents, but the number of incidents included in each
> individual dataset varies from **57 to 2955** incidents. Only **25** incidents (0.008% of all incidents) are
> included in all five datasets."

Spread across five datasets over 2013-2020: **2955 / 57 = 51.8x**; consensus core **25 / 3155**.
**Arithmetic correction to the primary [this memo's calculation]:** 25 / 3155 = 0.00792 = **0.79 %**, not the
"0.008%" printed in the abstract — the authors reported the *fraction* with a percent sign, understating their
own headline by **100x**. A paper about definitional data quality carries a unit slip in its own abstract.
Cite the ratio, not their percentage.
**The definitions themselves (primary):** Gun Violence Archive methodology page (gunviolencearchive.org/methodology,
read 2026-09-16) sets a purely numerical bar — four or more shot **or** killed, excluding the perpetrator, with
no context filter (public/private, premeditated/spontaneous, felony/gang all included), and GVA states its
inclusion philosophy directly: "The overall goal is to provide information on most types of gun violence, and
gun crime, no matter where it is on the political table." The CRS-derived family (USA TODAY/AP/Northeastern,
The Violence Project) requires four or more **killed** by firearm in a single event and separates public from
private location. Congress codified "mass killing" as **three or more** murdered in a single incident in a
public place (post-Sandy Hook, 2013) and extended the three-fatality threshold to "mass shootings" in **2022**;
Mother Jones then amended its own database from a four- to a three-fatality threshold (Rockefeller Institute,
Schildkraut et al., 2025-07-22). So the count that gets quoted also moved because **Congress changed the
definition in 2022** — statutory definitional expansion, item 46 in its purest form.
**Both directions:** the wide-definition count (GVA) serves gun-control advocacy and is transparently
documented; the narrow-definition count (FBI active shooter / MJ) serves rebuttal and is equally transparently
documented. Neither is fabricated. The politicized act is quoting one count while implying the other's
event-type.

### C-41.4 Flaxman et al. "3.1 million deaths averted" and the last-NPI parameter — GROUNDED (grade A both sides)
**Date:** *Nature* 2020-06-08; Matters Arising and Reply both 2020-12-23.
**Primary #1:** Flaxman S. et al., "Estimating the effects of non-pharmaceutical interventions on COVID-19 in
Europe", *Nature* 584, 257-261 (2020), DOI 10.1038/s41586-020-2405-7, full text read 2026-09-16 at nature.com.
The headline:
> "We find that across 11 countries **3.1 (2.8–3.5) million deaths have been averted** owing to interventions

**What the model attributes by construction — the authors' own Methods, verbatim:**
> "For all countries, interventions are **assumed to have the same relative effect on R_t** and are informed by
> mortality data across all countries. The only exception is that we use partial pooling to introduce
> country-specific effects of the effectiveness of **the last intervention introduced** in the study period in a
> country (**which is usually lockdown**)."

> "Partial pooling operates on the last intervention, which is—in most cases—lockdown."

> "Our parametric form of R_t assumes that **changes in R_t are an immediate response to interventions rather
> than gradual changes in behaviour**, and it does not include importation or subnational variation."

and on the counterfactual that produces the 3.1 million:
> "By comparing the deaths predicted under the model with no interventions to the deaths predicted in our
> intervention model, we calculated the total deaths averted in our study period."

> "When looking at **simplistic counterfactual models** over the whole epidemic, the number of potential deaths
> averted is substantial."

The authors call their own counterfactual "simplistic" in the Discussion. Two construction choices do the work:
(i) R_t can only move when an intervention occurs (a step function), so any decline in transmission *must* be
assigned to an intervention rather than to voluntary behaviour change; (ii) the only country-specific slot in the
model is attached to whichever intervention came **last**.

> **individual effectiveness of these NPIs cannot be reliably quantified**."

> "additional flexibility was introduced by **ascribing a country-specific effect to the NPI that was introduced
> last** in each country. This replaced the parameterization in a preprint version (Imperial College Report 13),

> ban in the other 10 countries (less than 2% mean reduction of R_t** and less than 15% with 95% credibility).
> Notably, the estimated effectiveness of the public events ban in Sweden is comparable to that of lockdown in
> the 10 countries in which one was implemented."

> "As lockdown was the last intervention in most countries, its estimated effect comprises a pooled effect
> (**82% mean reduction of R_t**) and a separate country-specific 'last NPI' effect (mean change in R_t of

### C-03.1 Police shootings: per-encounter vs lifetime cumulative risk — GROUNDED (grade A on two of three)
**Primary #1 — the per-encounter denominator.** Fryer R.G. Jr, "An Empirical Analysis of Racial Differences in
Police Use of Force", NBER Working Paper 22399, July 2016 (rev. Jan 2018). PDF read 2026-09-16 from
nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22399/w22399.pdf. Abstract:
> "On non-lethal uses of force, blacks and Hispanics are more than fifty percent more likely to experience some
> form of force **in interactions with police**. … On the most extreme use of force – officer-involved shootings –
> **we find no racial differences in either the raw data or when contextual factors are taken into account**."

The denominator is stated flatly in the data section: "Unless otherwise noted, **all results are conditional on
an interaction**." Fryer then writes the objection to his own denominator, in a dedicated section headed
"A Note on Potential Selection into Police Data Sets":
> "to the extent that there are racial differences in the probability of an interaction with police, these data
> may omit a very important margin. Put differently, **one may discover no differences in police use of force,
> conditional on an interaction, but large racial differences in the probability of the types of interactions in
> which force may be used.** By only concentrating on how and whether force was used in an interaction and
> ignoring whether or not an interaction took place, **one can misrepresent the total experience with police**."

Note also that the paper's two findings point opposite ways — a >50 % disparity in non-lethal force per
interaction, and no disparity in shootings — and the headline that circulated was only the second.
**Primary #2 — the lifetime-cumulative denominator.** Edwards F., Lee H. & Esposito M., "Risk of being killed by
police use of force in the United States by age, race–ethnicity, and sex", *PNAS* 116(34):16793-16798 (2019),
DOI 10.1073/pnas.1821204116, read 2026-09-16 via PMC6708348:
> Black men face "about a **1 in 1,000 chance of being killed by police over the life course**."

> "The average **lifetime odds** of being killed by police are about **1 in 2,000 for men** and about
> **1 in 33,000 for women**."

Method: **period life tables** built on Fatal Encounters data for 2013-2018, i.e. a cumulative risk over a
synthetic cohort's whole lifespan under the assumption that the 2013-2018 age-specific hazards persist.
**This memo's arithmetic on the construction:** a 1-in-1,000 *lifetime* risk is not a 1-in-1,000 annual or
per-encounter risk. Spread across a ~75-year synthetic lifespan, the *average* annual risk implied is about
**1 in 75,000**. The paper's own finding is that the hazard is strongly age-concentrated (peaking in the
mid-20s to mid-30s), so the annual risk at peak age is materially higher than that average and the risk at most
other ages is materially lower — which is exactly why the paper reports a lifetime figure and not an annual one.
The politicized move is dropping "over the life course": the same three words separate a cumulative-over-75-years
statistic from a rate.
**Primary #3 — the benchmark denominator (Cesario, Johnson & Terrill 2019): NOT REACHED this epoch.** Their
construction replaces the population denominator with a crime-involvement or arrest benchmark, which is itself
contested because arrest rates are an enforcement output rather than an offence measure. Grade **D** until
fetched. Query: `Cesario Johnson Terrill 2019 "Is There Evidence of Racial Disparity in Police Use of Force
During Arrests" Social Psychological and Personality Science benchmark violent crime arrests denominator` plus
the 2020 correction/retraction correspondence around Johnson et al. PNAS 2019.
**Both directions, same paragraph:** the per-encounter denominator (Fryer) answers "given a stop, is force
applied differently?" and is used in rebuttal of disparity claims, but it cannot see disparity in who gets
stopped — a limitation Fryer states himself, in his own paper, in a section written for that purpose, so the
rebuttal use of his shooting result routinely omits the author's own caveat **and** omits his own >50 % non-lethal
finding in the same abstract. **And** the lifetime-risk denominator (Edwards et al.) answers "over a whole life,
what is the cumulative chance?" and is used in support of disparity claims, but it is quoted as though it were a
current rate, and its life-table construction assumes 2013-2018 hazards hold for 75 years. Each denominator
answers a real question; each is quoted for a question it does not answer.
**Political use with the qualifier dropped: NOT REACHED as a primary.** Query:
`Congressional Record 2020 George Floyd Justice in Policing Act floor debate "1 in 1000" black men killed by
police` — a named-officeholder instance was not fetched, so do not attribute one.
**Direction:** Fryer's shooting result served the rebuttal side; the 1-in-1,000 figure served the disparity side.
The construction fault is symmetric and is item 3 (per-what) plus item 9 (window/exposure base).
