### CASE 47-A — Two opposing economists' letters on the same bill, one day apart (GROUNDED both directions, grade A)

Signatories include Nobel laureate **Vernon Smith**, former CEA chair Kevin Hassett, former OMB director Jim Miller, former Fed governor Robert Heller.
- **The construction problem, stated symmetrically.** Neither letter contains a magnitude, a horizon, a counterfactual, or a standard error. "Downward pressure on inflation" and "the exact opposite effect" are both directional claims with no quantity attached, so neither is falsifiable by any subsequent series. The credential inventory (Nobels, Clark medals, former office) is doing the persuasive work that a point estimate with an interval would do in a paper by the same authors. Both sides fielded a Nobel laureate (Stiglitz; Vernon Smith), which is itself the measurement: laureate-count does not discriminate between opposed positions on this question.

  | Aug 2023 | 307.026 | **3.67%** |
  | Aug 2024 | 314.796 | **2.53%** |
  | Aug 2022 → Aug 2024 (cumulative) | — | **6.29%** |

  Inflation fell sharply over the two years after passage. **This does not vindicate the supporting letter.** The series has no counterfactual in it, the Federal Reserve raised the target rate over the same window, and neither letter named a channel, a lag, or an effect size that the CPI could confirm or refute. ### CASE 48-A — Nature Human Behaviour makes "potential harm" a publication criterion (GROUNDED, grade A)
- **Mechanism:** 48 (journal politicization). **Date:** 2022-08-18.
- **Claim as used:** that a journal may refuse, correct, retract or amend research about human groups on grounds of *potential social harm to people who are not research participants*.
- **Political use:** cited in both directions in the 2022–2024 academic-freedom argument; the journal itself frames it as ethics guidance binding on all content types.
- **Primary record** (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-022-01443-2, editorial "Science must respect the dignity and rights of all humans", *Nature Human Behaviour* 6:1029–1031; read 2026-09-16). Operative text, verbatim:
  - "New ethics guidance addresses potential harms for human population groups who do not participate in research but may be harmed by its publication."
  - "Although academic freedom is fundamental, it is not unbounded."
  - "In some cases, however, potential harms to the populations studied may outweigh the benefit of publication."
  - "in cases of substantial risk of harm that outweighs any potential benefits, may decline publication (or correct, retract, remove or otherwise amend already published content)"
  - "Regardless of content type (research, review or opinion) and, for research, **regardless of whether a research project was reviewed and approved by an appropriate institutional ethics committee**, editors reserve the right to request modifications to (or correct or otherwise amend post-publication), and in severe cases refuse publication of (or retract post-publication): 1. Content that is premised upon the assumption of inherent biological, social, or cultural superiority or inferiority of one human group over [another]"
  - The guidance also instructs authors to follow "guidance on bias-free language provided by the American Psychological Association".
- **What the record shows about construction:** the criterion is *not* a truth criterion. It is a harm criterion applied post-hoc and explicitly independent of prior institutional ethics approval, and it reaches opinion and review content. No threshold, no measurement instrument for "harm", and no appeal mechanism is specified in the editorial.
- **Direction:** the operative categories (superiority/inferiority of a human group, stigmatizing language) bind hardest on research into group differences; in practice the constraint is asymmetric by topic, not by party. Page metrics as displayed on 2026-09-16: 91k accesses, 46 citations, Altmetric 1156.
- **[INFERENCE]** A journal-level harm veto converts a contested empirical question into an editorial-discretion question; it changes who decides, not what is true.

- **Mechanism:** 52. **Primary:** Kennedy & Tyson, "Public Trust in Scientists and Views on Their Role in Policymaking," Pew Research Center, 2024-11-14, https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2024/11/14/public-trust-in-scientists-and-views-on-their-role-in-policymaking/ (fielded 2024-10-21 to 10-27, **N = 9,593** U.S. adults). Read 2026-09-16 from the page source, not a summary.
- **Verbatim figures:**
  - Overall Oct 2024: "either a great deal (**26%**) or a fair amount (**51%**) of confidence in scientists to act in the best interests of the public. This share is up slightly since last year."
  - Peak: "In April 2020… **87%** expressed at least a fair amount of confidence in scientists, including **39%** who said they had a great deal of confidence."
  - Democrats/lean Oct 2024: "**88%** express a great deal or fair amount… The share of Democrats with at least a fair amount of confidence in scientists is **similar to levels seen prior to the pandemic**. However, the share of Democrats who express a great deal of confidence in scientists stands at **40%**… In April 2020, **52%** of Democrats expressed a great deal of confidence… and in November 2020, that share reached **55%**."
  - Republicans/lean Oct 2024: "**Two-thirds** (66%)… About a third (**34%**) express distrust, saying they have not too much or no confidence at all… In April 2020, an **85%** majority of Republicans said they had a great deal or fair amount of confidence in scientists, compared with just **14%** who had little or no confidence."
  - Movement: "Republicans' overall level of confidence in scientists is **up 5 percentage points** compared with a year ago – the first uptick in trust among Republicans since the start of the pandemic."
- **Computed from the above (stated calculation on sourced numbers):**

  | Measure | Apr/Nov 2020 | Oct 2024 | Change |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Republicans, great deal + fair amount | 85% (Apr 2020) | 66% | **−19 pts** |
  | Democrats, great deal + fair amount | — (Pew: "similar to pre-pandemic") | 88% | ≈flat |
  | Democrats, great deal only | 55% (Nov 2020) | 40% | **−15 pts** |
  | Overall, at least fair amount | 87% (Apr 2020) | 77% (26+51) | **−10 pts** |
  | Partisan gap (at least fair amount) | 2 pts (85 vs 87 overall) | **22 pts** (88 vs 66) | widened |

- **What this shows:** the decline is **not** uniform and it is **not** only Republican. Republicans lost 19 points of any-confidence; Democrats held any-confidence flat but lost 15 points of *strong* confidence from the Nov 2020 peak. The headline "trust in science collapsed among Republicans" is true on the any-confidence measure and misses the Democratic strong-confidence drop, which is of comparable size on its own measure. The 2020 peak is a pandemic-rally artifact, so any "decline since 2020" framing is measured against an abnormal baseline — the correct comparison for Democrats is pre-pandemic, against which Pew says they are unchanged.
- **Direction:** the "Republicans reject science" framing is served by the 85→66 series; the "the pandemic response cost science its authority across the board" framing is served by the 55→40 series. Both are in the same table.
- **Lane pointer:** the causal attribution study (Zhang 2023, *Nature Human Behaviour*) is held by lane B — cite by pointer, not redone here. A second causal study: see Disconfirmation record / NOT FOUND.
