Abstract
Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) enables LLM agents to improve continuously
from environment rewards, yet the resulting policies do not systematically accumulate reusable strategies that generalize across tasks. Modular skills can provide such
reusable strategies, yet existing skill-augmented RL methods decouple skill creation
from policy optimization, risking adopting skills that conflict with the evolving policy.
Inspired by Anthropic’s Skill Creator, we introduce R E S KILL, an RL-in-the-loop skill
creation framework that reconciles skill evolution with policy learning. R E S KILL exploits the group-wise structure of GRPO to naturally embed three mechanisms with
only marginal additional overhead: (1) an assertion-driven skill creator that diagnoses
failures from past experience and proposes conditional, trigger-based skill revisions;
(2) within-group rollout sampling that enables controlled comparison of skill versions,
capturing which version best supports the policy’s ongoing learning; and (3) Thompson
Sampling with adaptive discounting to balance exploration and exploitation in skill
version selection as the policy evolves. Across several domains, R E S KILL consistently
outperforms existing memory and skill-based RL methods, with the largest gains on
unseen tasks. Analysis of the skill lifecycle shows skills being automatically created,
tested, refined, and pruned as the policy improves, demonstrating reconciled skill-policy
co-evolution.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled autonomous agents capable of multi-turn interaction with
complex environments (Yao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Agentic reinforcement learning (RL), where
agents are trained from verifiable environment rewards, has emerged as a promising paradigm (Jin
et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Da et al., 2025), yet the resulting policies do not systematically accumulate
transferable problem-solving strategies across tasks. Recent work has shown that modular skills, reusable
snippets encoding task-specific strategies, can fill this gap (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2025; Ni et al.,
2026). A pioneering effort is Anthropic’s Skill Creator (Anthropic, 2026), which automates skill creation
through an iterative human-in-the-loop testing process (Figure 1(a)). However, the Skill Creator operates
on a static policy at inference time and relies on human feedback. A natural question arises: can we build
an RL-in-the-loop skill creation process that dynamically creates and evaluates skills alongside an evolving policy?
Recent approaches accumulate experiential knowledge during RL training through memory evolution
(Zhang et al., 2026), principle distillation (Wu et al., 2025), and skill distillation (Xia et al., 2025). However,

may not align with the evolving policy. Ideally, whether a skill benefits learning should be assessed by
testing it during policy training itself.
In this paper, we propose R E S KILL, a framework that reconciles skill creation with policy learning.
R E S KILL embeds the Anthropic Skill Creator cycle into the RL training loop and makes use of the groupwise structure of GRPO (Shao et al., 2024): each training step samples a group of rollouts from the same
task under the same policy, allowing us to assign competing skill versions within the same group and
directly compare their effects. To orchestrate this allocation, Thompson Sampling (Thompson, 1933) with
adaptive discounting is introduced to balance exploration and exploitation in skill version control as the
policy evolves. Each rollout thus serves a triple role: (i) policy optimization via GRPO gradients, (ii) failure
diagnosis and skill revision through an assertion-driven creator adapted from Anthropic’s Skill Creator,
and (iii) bandit evaluation that accepts or rejects skill versions based on how they support the policy’s
ongoing learning. Our method introduces marginal training overhead and requires no additional rollout
budget.
We compare R E S KILL against existing memory and skill-based agentic RL baselines on embodied reasoning (ALFWorld) and agentic search (seven benchmarks) across multiple model scales, with additional
experiments on coding (InterCode-SQL), web shopping (WANDS), and science discovery (ScienceWorld).
R E S KILL achieves substantially improved performance, with the largest gains on unseen and hard tasks.
The skill lifecycle analysis shows skills being automatically created, tested, refined, and pruned as the
policy improves. Furthermore, the co-evolved policy can adapt to new domains (ALFWorld to ScienceWorld) at test time through skill creation alone, indicating that reconciled training develops a general
capacity to follow and benefit from new skills.

3.1

RL Training with Within-Group Skill Testing

Within-Group Sampling. To evaluate whether a skill benefits the evolving policy, we need to observe
how the policy behaves under it. However, dedicated testing rollouts are expensive in multi-turn settings,

structure to address both issues: for each rollout i in a group, a version assignment vi is drawn from the
Thompson Sampling posterior (§3.3), and the rollout is generated under the corresponding skill bank
Svi . The R E S KILL objective extends JGRPO by replacing the static skill bank S with a version-dependent

Triple-Duty Rollouts. This formulation allows each rollout to simultaneously serves three purposes

3.2

RL-in-the-Loop Skill Creation

The within-group sampling (§3.1) presents a cost free skill evaluation mechanism; this section describes
the skill creator pipeline that produces candidate skill versions for evaluation. We adapt the diagnosisproposal-validation loop inspired by Anthropic’s Skill Creator (Anthropic, 2026), replacing human
feedback with evolving policy trajectories and verifiable reward signals from the RL training loop. Full
details are in Appendix C.3 and C.4.
Experience Reservoir. The original Skill Creator requires a human practitioner to supply execution
examples for diagnosis. We replace this manual step with a continuously refreshed experience reservoir
R that collects success-failure trajectory pairs from ongoing RL training, each trajectory is annotated
with its skill version label vi and reward r (τi ). Since R evolves alongside the policy, it always reflects the
current behavioral landscape and provides the raw material for the diagnosis-proposal loop described
below.
Conditional Skill Loading. Each skill Sk = (ck , trigk ) is composed of structured content ck (situational
applicability, action guidance, and counterexamples) with an iteratively refined trigger condition trigk
parameterized by type. At each step, only skills whose triggers fire are loaded into the context, yielding
the active subset of S . Each loaded skill includes a when_to_use field that describes when its guidance
should be followed, allowing the policy to decide whether the triggered skill is relevant to the current
state.
Assertion-Based Failure Grading. Rather than only diagnosing individual trajectories, we also build
a general failure profile across the entire reservoir. A dynamic assertion set A = {ϕj } of rule-based
predicates is applied uniformly to all trajectories, yielding per-assertion pass rates r̂ j = |R|−1 ∑τ ∈R ϕj (τ ).
An LLM-based analyzer receives these pass rates together with a stratified sample of trajectories and
produces two outputs: (1) a prevalence-ranked failure diagnosis that guides skill revision, and (2) updates
to A itself to keep the assertion set aligned with the policy’s shifting failure landscape.
4

History-Informed Skill Revision. Given the current skill bank, failure diagnosis with representative
episodes, and the tracked history of prior proposals with their accept/reject outcomes, a skill creator
agent proposes a candidate version Snew : a coherent set of ADD, MODIFY, and DELETE operations. Before
entering testing, each proposed trigger’s firing rate is validated against the reservoir to ensure sufficient
activation coverage. We now have the newly proposed Snew and Sold . In the next section, we introduce
how rollouts are allocated between Snew and Sold , and when to accept or reject.

Policy-Aware Posterior Update. As the policy evolves, recent steps provide the most informative signal
about a skill version’s current effectiveness, but a single step may lack sufficient samples for a reliable
estimate. We therefore aggregate across the evaluation window but discount earlier evidence as the
policy evolves. At each step t, existing pseudo-counts are shrunk by wt before incorporating mt successes
out of nt new episodes:

where v ∈ {new, old} and M > 0 is a memory parameter. Since Thompson Sampling allocates different
sample sizes to each version, the discount adapts accordingly: a version with fewer observations (nt ≪ M)
retains more prior evidence (wt → 1), while one with many observations (nt ≫ M) can aggressively
discount the past (wt → 0).
The optimal value of M varies across settings. We estimate M from data via sequential predictivelikelihood maximization over completed evaluation cycles (Appendix C.5). At each cycle boundary, the
system selects the M that best explains the observed reward sequence under the discounted Beta model,
requiring no manual tuning.

Experiments

4.1

Setup

Environments. We evaluate on five domains spanning embodied, retrieval, and tool-use reasoning.
(1) Embodied reasoning: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), a text-based household environment evaluated
on seen and unseen task configurations. (2) Agentic search: agents answer factual questions via iterative
search and reasoning. We train on NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), and
evaluate generalization on five unseen datasets: PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017),
2WikiMHQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and Bamboogle (Trivedi et al., 2023). (3) Science
discovery, coding, and web shopping: we further extend to ScienceWorld (electricity tasks) (Wang et al.,
2022), InterCode-SQL (Yang et al., 2023), and WANDS (Chen et al., 2022) to assess broad applicability.
Baselines. We compare R E S KILL against four categories of methods. (1) Baselines: ReAct base model
and closed-source model (Claude 4.5 Sonnet). (2) Evolvement methods: memory and procedure evolution
without policy training, including R EME (Cao et al., 2025) and M EMRL (Zhang et al., 2026). (3) Skill-only
baselines: using the oracle skill bank from S KILLRL (Xia et al., 2025), we test retrieval-based loading
(BM25, HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., 2024)) and full skill loading (long-context) on the base model without
RL. (4) RL-based methods: G RPO (Shao et al., 2024) (no skills), M EMRL (+RL), E VOLVER (Wu et al., 2025),
I NSPO (Zhou et al., 2026), and S KILLRL.
Implementation Details. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) as base
models; unless otherwise specified, results report the 4B model. For fair comparison, all evolve, skill,
RL-based methods and R E S KILL use the same Claude 4.5 Sonnet for skill or memory creation (base-model
alternative ablated in Table 2). For R E S KILL, we set the evolution frequency Ke =5 and skill bank size to 8
(sensitivity analysis in Appendix C.7). Full training hyperparameters are in Appendix B.1.

Main Results

Table 1 summarizes performance across benchmarks, model scales, and seen/unseen splits; Figure 4
shows validation curves during training, comparing R E S KILL against G RPO, skill-augmented RL methods,
and two key ablation variants.
Consistent Gains Across Benchmarks and Scales. R E S KILL outperforms all baselines on both benchmarks at both model scales (Table 1). Non-RL methods trail by 22.0–32.3% on ALFWorld and 13.1–14.6%
on Search, underscoring the importance of policy optimization; R E S KILL lifts over vanilla G RPO by 3.0–
14.5%, demonstrating the value of skill guidance. Most importantly, R E S KILL outperforms the strongest
memory and skill-augmented RL baseline by 5.9%/6.9% on ALFWorld and 2.5%/2.3% on Search (4B/8B),
a gap attributable to reconciling skill evolution with the evolving policy rather than treating the two as
independent processes.
Amplified Gains on Unseen Tasks. The gains are particularly strong on unseen tasks. On ALFWorld (8B),
R E S KILL achieves 95.3% on unseen tasks, a 12.7% gap over S KILLRL (82.6%) that is substantially larger
than its seen-split margin (1.2%). On Search, R E S KILL’s improvement over S KILLRL on unseen splits is
about 2.1× larger than on seen splits. Figure 4 reveals that methods track closely in early training, but
R E S KILL pulls ahead on unseen splits in later stages, presumably because reconciled evolution retains
only skill versions that generalize better.
Broad Applicability. The pattern of amplified gains on harder and out-of-domain tasks is further
validated across three extension domains (Figure 3). On ScienceWorld, R E S KILL reaches 48.8% on OOD
tasks, nearly doubling S KILLRL (26.9%), whose static skill bank degrades sharply on unseen task variants.
On InterCode-SQL, methods perform comparably on Hard queries, but R E S KILL pulls ahead on Extrahard (63.6% vs. 55.3% S KILLRL), where compositional SQL reasoning benefits most from evolved skills.
WANDS shows consistent gains across optimization horizons, with R E S KILL reaching 86.6% on the harder
3-opt setting vs. S KILLRL’s 82.1%. Across all three domains, the gap between R E S KILL and the strongest
baseline widens as task difficulty increases.

capacity.
Ablation Study. Table 2 isolates the contribution of each remaining component. (1) Co-evolution order: replacing simultaneous co-evolution with sequential alternatives (skill-first or policy-first) drops
performance by 10.4–18.5% on ALFWorld and 1.7–2.1% on Search, showcasing the importance of joint
co-evolution rather than separate stages. (2) Adaptive weighting: replacing the recency discount (Eq. 4)
with last-step only (wt =0) drops by 12.9%/0.9%, as a single step provides too few samples for reliable
skill version selection. Uniform weighting (wt =1) narrows the gap to 3.3%/0.6% but does not account
for policy drift, treating stale evidence equally with recent observations even as the policy evolves.
(3) Assertion-based analyzer: removing it in favor of direct per-episode LLM diagnosis costs 5.1%/0.9%,
showing that a general failure profile across the reservoir yields more targeted skill revisions than
individual trajectory inspection.

5

Related Work

We discuss the most closely related work here; broader context on reinforcement learning for LLMs,
tool-integrated agents, and bandit methods is provided in Appendix D.
Prompt and Skill Optimization. The performance of LLM agents is highly sensitive to their guiding
instructions (Zhou et al., 2025). Automated prompt optimization approaches use paraphrasing (Zhou
et al., 2023), LLM-based optimizers (Yang et al., 2024), or textual gradients (Pryzant et al., 2023; Yüksekgönül et al., 2025) to improve instructions, but operate offline. In the skill domain, prior work extracts
experiential rules from trajectories (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024), builds open-ended skill libraries
(Wang et al., 2023), and distills reusable workflows from agent experience (Wang et al., 2024). Anthropic’s
Skill Creator (Anthropic, 2026) automates the creation of modular, trigger-based skills with controlled
blind testing, but cannot be directly integrated into a reinforcement learning process. These methods
produce valuable skills but do not reconcile creation with policy training.
Memory and Skill-Based Reinforcement Learning. Recent work brings skill and memory optimization
into RL training: M EMRL (Zhang et al., 2026) evolves episodic memory alongside policy training,
E VOLVER (Wu et al., 2025) self-distills experience principles retrieved during RL, S KILLRL (Xia et al.,
2025) distills teacher-model skills with trigger-based activation, and I NSPO (Zhou et al., 2026) maintains
instruction candidates sampled per rollout group. However, all these approaches treat skill creation and
policy learning as separate processes. R E S KILL closes this gap by reconciling skill evolution with policy
training through within-group testing and reward-based selection.

6

Conclusion

We introduced R E S KILL, a framework that reconciles skill creation with policy learning by embedding
assertion-driven skill revision, within-group skill testing, and adaptive Thompson Sampling into the
10

G RPO training loop at zero additional rollout cost. Across five benchmark domains, R E S KILL consistently
outperforms existing skill and memory-based RL methods, with the largest gains on unseen tasks.
Limitations and future directions are discussed in Appendix A.

Limitations and Future Work

Evolution frequency. As shown in the sensitivity analysis (Figure 9, Appendix C.7), performance on
ALFWorld degrades when evolution frequency is too low (Ke =20). We observe that infrequent evolution
causes the policy to adapt to a fixed skill context; subsequent skill updates then introduce distribution
shift that leads to training instability. The evolution frequency remains a hyperparameter requiring
per-domain tuning, and we view adaptive evolution scheduling as a promising future direction.
Skill invocation. Our trigger-based design loads skills into context whenever trigger conditions match,
which avoids additional inference steps but gives the model less control over when skills are applied. A
natural alternative is to formulate skills as explicit tool calls, providing fine-grained invocation control
at the cost of extra generation steps per decision point. Exploring tool-call formulations that maintain
rollout efficiency, for example through batched or amortized skill invocation, is a promising direction.
Skill capacity burden. Adding skills to the context increases the cognitive load on the base model,
and we observe two failure modes in practice (see case studies in Appendix F). First, smaller models
sometimes conflate skill guidance with environment actions, emitting skill names as if they were executable commands rather than incorporating the skill’s advice into their reasoning. Second, models
may over-apply a skill in situations where it is inapplicable, lacking the capacity to recognize when to
disengage. Both failures suggest that skill-augmented RL would benefit from training the policy to treat
skills as reasoning-time guidance rather than action-time directives, for example through format-aware
reward shaping or explicit separation of skill invocation from action generation.

RL-Based Methods. E VOLVER (Wu et al., 2025) operates in alternating cycles of online interaction

stored in a curated experience base with semantic deduplication. During training, the agent retrieves
relevant principles via embedding similarity to guide its reasoning. Principle quality is tracked via
Laplace-smoothed success rates, with low-scoring principles pruned periodically.
I NSPO (Zhou et al., 2026) maintains a population of instruction candidates sampled per rollout group

of recent rewards. The population is periodically pruned and replenished by an LLM-based optimizer

that reflects on failure trajectories and proposes new candidates. At inference, only the highest-weight
instruction is used.
S KILLRL (Xia et al., 2025) distills modular skills from training trajectories using a teacher LLM, where
each skill consists of a title, an actionable principle, and a triggering condition. Skills are injected into the
policy’s context via template-based retrieval during both training and evaluation. After validation epochs,
failed trajectories trigger recursive skill addition: the teacher identifies unaddressed failure patterns and

B.3

Benchmark Details

ALFWorld. ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) is an embodied reasoning benchmark where agents interact
with household environments via text commands. The action space consists of approximately 14 verb
templates (e.g., go to, take, put, open, examine), with a maximum horizon of 50 steps and binary reward
(10 on task completion, 0 otherwise). We train on approximately 3,800 games and group the six task types
into three categories: Pick&Place, Transform (averaging Heat, Cool, and Clean), and Examine. Evaluation
uses 140 seen and 134 unseen game configurations; we report per-category success rates, seen/unseen
averages (micro-averaged over all games in each split), and an overall success rate weighted by split size.
Agentic Search. We follow the multi-dataset evaluation protocol of Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025). Agents
answer factual questions via iterative retrieval: the action space is two actions (issue a search query or
submit a final answer), with a maximum of 4 turns and binary reward (1 on exact match, 0 otherwise).
Retrieval uses E5-base-v2 dense embeddings over a Wikipedia corpus (∼18M passages), returning the
top-3 passages per query. We train on ∼3,000 questions from NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) and HotpotQA
(Yang et al., 2018). Seen evaluation datasets: NQ (300) and HotpotQA (300). Unseen datasets: PopQA (Mallen
et al., 2023) (303), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) (300), 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020) (300), MuSiQue
(Trivedi et al., 2022) (300), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023) (125). We report per-dataset accuracy,
seen/unseen averages weighted by dataset size, and overall accuracy across all 1,928 examples.
WANDS (Web Shopping). We construct a web shopping environment inspired by WebShop (Yao et al.,
2022), built from the WANDS product search dataset (Chen et al., 2022) due to licensing concerns with the
original WebShop data. Agents must find and purchase a product matching a goal specification (category,
attributes, configurable options, price constraint). The action space is two actions (search or click), with a
maximum of 15 steps and binary reward (10 if the correct product is purchased with all required options
selected, 0 otherwise). Tasks are categorized by the number of options that must be correctly selected:
2-opt (two required options) and 3-opt (three required options, harder). Training uses 3,000 products
from the Furniture domain; evaluation uses 496 held-out products spanning both seen (Furniture) and
unseen (Kitchen & Tabletop) domains. We report accuracy stratified by option complexity (2-opt and
3-opt).
ScienceWorld. ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022) is an interactive science simulation where agents perform
multi-step experiments. We use the electricity split, which contains two task types: identifying power
components and testing conductivity. The action space consists of approximately 26 verb templates
(e.g., pick up, pour, activate, connect, focus on), with a maximum of 50 steps and binary reward (10
if task score exceeds 70%, 0 otherwise). For in-distribution evaluation (Test-ID), we use the dev split
of 230 episodes with the same task types but held-out variations; for out-of-distribution evaluation
(Test-OOD), we use 155 episodes from related but distinct task variants (conductivity with unknown
materials, renewable energy). We report ID and OOD accuracy.
InterCode-SQL. InterCode-SQL (Yang et al., 2023) evaluates interactive SQL generation over Spider
databases (Yu et al., 2018). Agents iteratively write and execute SQL queries with execution feedback
before submitting a final answer; the action space is two actions (execute exploratory query or submit),
17

with a maximum of 10 turns and binary reward (10 on execution result match with the ground truth, 0
otherwise). Following SQL-Trail (Hua et al., 2026), training uses 1,027 difficulty-balanced queries, filtered
via pass@6 to exclude trivially solvable and impossible cases. Evaluation uses 1,000 examples from 20
held-out Spider databases.

C.3

Skill Creator Pipeline: Full Details

Inspired by Anthropic’s Skill Creator (Anthropic, 2026), our skill creator pipeline decomposes skill
creation into the following stages: reservoir collection, assertion grading, contrastive analysis, insight
aggregation and semantic grouping, skill recommendation, and skill authoring. We present each stage in
execution order.
C.3.1

Reservoir Collection and Contrastive Analysis

Each G RPO rollout produces trajectories tagged with a version assignment vi ∈ {Snew , Sold } and binary
reward r (τi ). The reservoir R stores these trajectories in a last-in-first-out structure, enforcing diversity
by retaining at most one success and one failure per version per task. That is, for each task the reservoir
holds up to four episodes (new-success, new-failure, old-success, old-failure), replacing older entries as
new rollouts arrive.
18

When skill creation is triggered, the reservoir is sampled into task groups. We prioritize groups with
diversity: those containing both successes and failures, and both active and inactive skill conditions. Each
group receives a separate LLM call comparing the episodes within it: success vs. failure, new-skill vs.
old-skill, and the specific actions at which behavior diverges. The LLM produces a structured insight per
group containing: a behavioral finding, a failure-mode label, the failure and success action snippets, an
assessment of whether active skills helped or hurt, and a confidence level. This step condenses the raw
reservoir into a manageable set of grounded insights for downstream diagnosis.
C.3.2

Assertion Grading and Diagnosis

Assertion Types. The assertion grader G maintains a dynamic set of rule-based assertions A = {ϕj },
where each assertion is a predicate over trajectories generated by the LLM-based diagnoser. Assertions
capture behavioral properties at varying granularity: whether a particular action pattern appears (or is
absent) in a trajectory, whether two actions occur in the correct temporal order (e.g., searching before
submitting), whether the agent avoids unproductive repetition, whether the trajectory falls within
expected length bounds, and whether the environment’s observations indicate the agent reached certain
states. The diagnoser creates, modifies, and deletes assertions as the policy’s failure landscape evolves.
Grading. Given the failure reservoir R, the grader evaluates every trajectory against all assertions,
producing per-assertion pass rates:

C.3.3

Skill Recommendation and Authoring

Recommender. The recommender receives the insight groups (with expanded representative insights
showing full action snippets), the current skill library with per-skill diagnostics (trigger rate, age), the
version history of prior proposals with their accept/reject outcomes, and the environment’s action
vocabulary. It proposes 1–3 operations (ADD, MODIFY, or DELETE), each specifying: the operation type,
intent (what to change and why), and supporting evidence (which insight group motivates it). The
recommender does not write skill content; it focuses purely on strategic decisions.
The recommender is also informed of the skill lifecycle stage: when the library is below capacity it favors
ADD operations to build coverage, and as skills accumulate toward Kmax it shifts toward MODIFY and
DELETE to refine and prune. Beyond lifecycle guidance, it prioritizes unaddressed failure clusters for
additions, skills whose guidance contradicts observed successful behavior for modifications, and skills
the policy has internalized (chronically low trigger rates or high reward regardless of skill presence) for
deletions.
Author. The author receives the recommended operations along with selected experience summaries and
available action patterns, focusing on creating the skill content.

C.4

Trigger Condition Types and Validation

C.4.1

Trigger Types

Each skill Sk = (ck , trigk ) has a deterministic trigger condition evaluated at every step of a rollout. The
trigger determines whether the skill’s content is loaded into the agent’s context at that step.
General Triggers. Skills with trigger_type = general fire at every step of every episode. These encode
broad strategic principles applicable regardless of the agent’s current state.
Beginning Triggers. Skills with trigger_type = beginning fire only at step t = 0 (episode initialization).
These provide planning-level guidance for how to approach the task without cluttering mid-episode
prompts.
Action-Pattern Triggers. Skills with trigger_type = action_pattern fire when the agent’s previous
action at−1 matches a regex pattern stored in trigger_pattern. For example, a skill targeting search
refinement might use trigger_pattern = "search\[.*\]" to activate only after the agent has just
performed a search action. This enables context-sensitive guidance: a skill about interpreting search
results only appears after a search, not during unrelated actions.

• When nt = M (the crossover point): wt = 1/2, so old pseudo-counts are halved and old and new
evidence contribute equally.
• When nt ≫ M (many new episodes): wt ≈ 0, so old evidence is aggressively discounted because the
new batch alone provides a reliable estimate.
We call M the memory parameter: it controls how many recent observations the system effectively remembers. A small M gives short memory (fast adaptation, more noise); a large M gives long memory (slow
adaptation, more stability).

required to estimate it. Each completed A/B test window produces a sequence of per-step observations (mt , nt ) for each arm. A well-chosen M should make the discounted posterior predictive: given

The discount weights reveal the adaptive allocation behavior. When Thompson Sampling favors the
new skill (allocating more episodes to it), the new arm’s wt drops (more forgetting) while the old arm’s
wt rises (more retention), because the old arm receives fewer episodes and its estimate is noisier. This
anti-correlation is visible throughout: around step 30, the new skill dominates allocation; conversely,
around step 175, the old skill dominates. The discount thus automatically gives more memory to the
under-sampled arm at each step.
